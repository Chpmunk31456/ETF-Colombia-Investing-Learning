# Lesson 6 — CDTs, Fixed Income, and Interest-Rate Risk

## Learning Objective

Understand how CDTs and fixed-income investments work, how yields are quoted, and why apparently “safe” investments can still carry inflation, liquidity, credit, reinvestment, and interest-rate risk.

## What Fixed Income Means

Fixed income generally refers to investments that promise contractual payments such as:

- Interest
- Principal repayment
- Periodic coupons
- A maturity value

Examples include:

- CDTs
- Government bonds
- Corporate bonds
- Money-market instruments
- Fixed-income collective investment funds
- Bond ETFs

The term “fixed income” does not mean the market price is fixed or that losses are impossible.

## What Is a CDT?

A CDT is a time deposit issued by a financial institution.

The investor deposits money for a defined period and receives interest according to the agreed terms.

Important variables include:

- Principal
- Interest rate
- Term
- Payment frequency
- Maturity date
- Early-withdrawal rules
- Tax withholding
- Deposit-insurance coverage
- Issuing institution

## Simple Interest Versus Compound Interest

### Simple interest

Interest is calculated only on the original principal.

### Compound interest

Interest is earned on:

- The original principal
- Previously accumulated interest

Compounding frequency can materially affect the final value.

Common compounding periods include:

- Monthly
- Quarterly
- Semiannual
- Annual

## Effective Annual Rate

The effective annual rate reflects the actual annual return after compounding.

When comparing investments, use consistent measures.

Do not compare:

- A monthly rate directly with an annual rate
- A nominal rate directly with an effective rate
- A before-tax return directly with an after-tax return

## Gross Return Versus Net Return

### Gross return

Return before:

- Taxes
- Fees
- Inflation
- Currency conversion
- Other costs

### Net return

Return after relevant costs and taxes.

For decision-making, the net real return is more informative.

## Nominal Return Versus Real Return

Nominal return is the stated percentage gain.

Real return adjusts for inflation.

Approximate relationship:

Real return ≈ nominal return minus inflation

Example:

- CDT nominal return: 10%
- Inflation: 7%
- Approximate real return before taxes: 3%

The exact calculation differs slightly because percentages compound.

## After-Tax Return

Interest income may be subject to withholding or income taxation.

The investor should record:

- Gross interest
- Withholding tax
- Other tax obligations
- Net cash received

A higher advertised rate may not produce the highest after-tax return.

## Maturity

Maturity is the date when the investment principal is scheduled to be repaid.

Short maturities may provide:

- Greater liquidity
- More frequent reinvestment opportunities
- Lower interest-rate sensitivity

Long maturities may provide:

- A longer locked-in rate
- Less frequent reinvestment
- Greater exposure to inflation and opportunity cost

## Reinvestment Risk

Reinvestment risk is the possibility that money received at maturity must be reinvested at a lower rate.

Example:

- A one-year CDT pays 10%
- At maturity, comparable CDTs pay only 6%

The investor cannot assume the original rate will continue.

## Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the benefit forgone by choosing one investment over another.

A long-term CDT may become unattractive if:

- Market rates rise
- Better investments become available
- The investor needs liquidity
- Inflation increases

## Liquidity Risk

Liquidity risk is the possibility that money cannot be accessed quickly without:

- Waiting until maturity
- Paying a penalty
- Selling at a discount
- Using a secondary market

Before opening a CDT, confirm whether:

- Early redemption is allowed
- The instrument can be endorsed or transferred
- A secondary market exists
- Penalties apply
- Partial withdrawals are possible

## Credit Risk

Credit risk is the possibility that the issuing institution or borrower cannot meet its obligations.

Review:

- Regulatory status
- Financial strength
- Credit rating
- Deposit-insurance eligibility
- Maximum protected amount
- Concentration with one institution

Deposit insurance does not necessarily protect every product or every amount.

## Inflation Risk

Inflation risk is the possibility that purchasing power declines faster than the investment grows.

A positive nominal return can still produce a negative real return.

Example:

- Nominal return: 8%
- Inflation: 10%

The investor gains pesos but loses purchasing power.

## Interest-Rate Risk

Interest-rate risk is the possibility that the market value of an existing bond declines when market rates rise.

Example:

- Existing bond pays 6%
- New bonds pay 10%

Investors may value the older 6% bond less because better rates are available elsewhere.

This matters most when selling before maturity.

## Bond Price and Yield Relationship

Bond prices and yields generally move in opposite directions.

- Interest rates rise → existing bond prices tend to fall
- Interest rates fall → existing bond prices tend to rise

A bond held to maturity may still repay according to its terms, assuming no default, but interim market values can fluctuate.

## Duration

Duration estimates how sensitive a bond or bond fund is to interest-rate changes.

General rule:

- Short duration → lower sensitivity
- Long duration → higher sensitivity

A bond ETF with long duration can experience substantial price declines when interest rates rise.

## Credit Quality

Credit quality reflects the issuer’s estimated ability to repay debt.

Common categories include:

- High-quality government debt
- Investment-grade corporate debt
- High-yield or speculative debt

Higher yields often compensate investors for greater credit or liquidity risk.

## Yield to Maturity

Yield to maturity estimates the annualized return if:

- The bond is held to maturity
- Payments occur as scheduled
- Coupons are reinvested at the assumed rate
- The issuer does not default

It is an estimate, not a guarantee.

## Current Yield

Current yield is generally:

Annual coupon payment divided by current market price

It does not include:

- Capital gain or loss at maturity
- Reinvestment assumptions
- Default risk
- Taxes
- Fees

## Bond Funds and Bond ETFs

A bond ETF usually has no single maturity date for the investor.

The fund continuously:

- Buys bonds
- Sells bonds
- Reinvests proceeds
- Maintains its target duration or strategy

This differs from holding an individual bond or CDT to maturity.

## CDT Versus Individual Bond

| Feature | CDT | Individual Bond |
|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Financial institution | Government or company |
| Maturity | Defined | Defined |
| Market price fluctuation | Usually not visible if held to maturity | Can fluctuate |
| Early access | Often restricted | May be sold if market exists |
| Credit risk | Institution | Issuer |
| Deposit insurance | May apply | Usually does not apply |
| Income structure | Agreed deposit rate | Coupon and/or discount |
| Complexity | Usually lower | Usually higher |

## CDT Versus Bond ETF

| Feature | CDT | Bond ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Principal maturity | Defined | No fixed investor maturity |
| Daily price changes | Usually not displayed | Yes |
| Liquidity | Limited by contract | Exchange-traded |
| Interest-rate sensitivity | Opportunity-cost risk | Visible market-price risk |
| Diversification | One institution unless laddered | Usually many bonds |
| Fees | Often embedded | Expense ratio and trading costs |
| Tax treatment | Interest income | Distributions and capital gains may apply |

## CDT Ladder Strategy

A ladder divides fixed-income money among several maturities.

Example:

- 25% in 90-day CDT
- 25% in 180-day CDT
- 25% in 270-day CDT
- 25% in 360-day CDT

Potential benefits:

- Regular liquidity
- Reduced dependence on one interest rate
- More frequent opportunities to reinvest
- Better matching of future expenses

Potential disadvantages:

- More administration
- Multiple maturity dates
- Possible lower rates on shorter terms
- Continued reinvestment risk

## Matching Investments to Expenses

Fixed-income maturities can be aligned with expected obligations.

Examples:

- School expenses
- Insurance premiums
- Vehicle costs
- Medical expenses
- Housing payments
- Emergency reserves

Money needed on a known date should not depend on selling a volatile investment.

## Currency Considerations

A Colombian-peso fixed-income product may be appropriate for future COP expenses.

A US-dollar fixed-income product may better match USD obligations.

Important questions:

- Which currency will be needed?
- What conversion costs apply?
- Could exchange-rate changes erase the yield advantage?
- Is the investor intentionally accepting currency risk?

## Institutional Diversification

Do not evaluate only the investment rate.

Also consider concentration across:

- Banks
- Brokers
- Fund managers
- Custodians
- Countries
- Currencies

Holding all fixed-income assets with one institution can create unnecessary concentration.

## Fixed-Income Due-Diligence Checklist

Before investing, record:

- Issuer
- Regulatory status
- Product type
- Principal amount
- Interest rate
- Effective annual rate
- Term
- Maturity date
- Payment frequency
- Early-withdrawal rules
- Tax withholding
- Deposit-insurance status
- Credit rating
- Currency
- Inflation assumption
- Net expected return
- Official source

## Beginner Rules

1. Compare effective annual rates, not incompatible quoted rates.
2. Evaluate after-tax and after-inflation returns.
3. Confirm early-withdrawal rules before investing.
4. Do not assume fixed income means no risk.
5. Match maturities to expected expenses.
6. Diversify institutions when appropriate.
7. Understand deposit-insurance limits.
8. Do not chase yield without examining credit risk.
9. Keep emergency funds liquid.
10. Record every maturity date and renewal instruction.
