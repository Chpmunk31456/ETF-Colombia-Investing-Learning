# Lesson 37 — Portfolio Rebalancing, Allocation Drift, and Tax-Aware Cash-Flow Controls

## Learning Objective

Learn how to measure portfolio drift, decide when rebalancing is necessary, use contributions and withdrawals efficiently, control transaction costs, preserve liquidity, and avoid unnecessary tax consequences.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

Tax treatment, transaction costs, settlement rules, pension restrictions, investment-account rules, and cross-border reporting obligations vary by jurisdiction, account, product, and investor.

Obtain current professional tax and investment advice before making a material taxable sale or cross-border portfolio change.

## Core Principle

Rebalancing is not market prediction.

Rebalancing restores the portfolio toward its approved risk structure after:

- Market gains
- Market losses
- Contributions
- Withdrawals
- Currency movements
- Property purchases
- Property sales
- Debt reduction
- Retirement spending
- Changes in financial circumstances

## Target Allocation

A target allocation specifies the intended percentage assigned to each asset category.

Possible categories include:

- Cash
- Emergency reserves
- COP fixed income
- USD fixed income
- Global equities
- Colombian equities
- Real estate
- Retirement accounts
- Other investments

The target should come from the written Investment Policy Statement.

## Current Allocation

Current allocation equals:

Current category value
divided by total portfolio value

Example:

- Global equities: COP 180 million
- Total portfolio: COP 300 million

Current global-equity allocation:

60%

## Allocation Drift

Allocation drift is the difference between:

Current allocation
and target allocation

Example:

- Target global equities: 50%
- Current global equities: 60%

Drift:

+10 percentage points

## Percentage Points Versus Percentage Change

A move from 50% to 60% is:

- 10 percentage points of allocation drift
- 20% relative increase from the original percentage

Use percentage points for portfolio allocation reporting.

## Why Drift Matters

Drift can change:

- Volatility
- drawdown exposure
- income production
- currency exposure
- liquidity
- concentration
- retirement risk
- property dependence

A portfolio can become more aggressive without any new purchase.

## Sources of Drift

Common sources include:

- Equity-market gains
- Bond-price changes
- Currency movement
- Property appreciation
- Property purchase
- large cash withdrawal
- pension payment accumulation
- dividend reinvestment
- automatic investment plans
- sale of one asset
- debt payoff
- inheritance
- business ownership changes

## Asset Categories

Use categories broad enough to support decision-making.

Example:

- Operating cash
- Emergency reserve
- Short-term fixed income
- Long-term fixed income
- Global equity
- Colombia equity
- Property
- Other

Avoid unnecessary categories that make the plan difficult to maintain.

## Account-Level Versus Household-Level Allocation

### Account-level allocation

Measures one account.

### Household-level allocation

Measures all relevant assets across:

- Banks
- Brokers
- retirement accounts
- CDTs
- property
- cash
- debt where applicable

A brokerage account may appear balanced while the total household is heavily concentrated in real estate.

## Gross Versus Net Exposure

Property exposure should preferably use net equity rather than gross property value when comparing it with net worth.

Approximate property equity equals:

Conservative property value
minus mortgage balance
minus estimated selling costs

## Restricted Assets

Some assets may be difficult or costly to rebalance.

Examples:

- Primary residence
- rental property
- restricted pension
- long-term CDT
- business ownership
- property under legal dispute

The liquid portfolio may need to compensate for illiquid concentration.

## Strategic Allocation

Strategic allocation reflects long-term objectives.

It should be based on:

- Time horizon
- reliable income
- essential expenses
- emergency reserves
- loss tolerance
- retirement needs
- property exposure
- currency needs
- tax considerations
- estate objectives

## Tactical Allocation

Tactical allocation makes temporary changes based on market views.

Tactical decisions can increase:

- trading
- timing risk
- tax cost
- behavioral error
- concentration

A beginner plan may prohibit tactical allocation unless explicitly approved.

## Rebalancing Methods

## Calendar Rebalancing

Review the portfolio on a fixed schedule.

Possible schedules:

- Quarterly
- semiannual
- annual

Advantages:

- Simple
- predictable
- reduces constant monitoring

Risks:

- Large drift may develop between reviews
- small unnecessary trades may occur

## Threshold Rebalancing

Rebalance when allocation crosses a defined limit.

Example:

- Target: 50%
- Permitted range: 45% to 55%

Advantages:

- Responds to material drift
- avoids small trades

Risks:

- Requires monitoring
- thresholds must be defined clearly

## Hybrid Rebalancing

Review on a fixed schedule and trade only when thresholds are breached.

Example:

- Review quarterly
- rebalance only when drift exceeds 5 percentage points

## Relative Threshold

A relative threshold is based on a percentage of the target.

Example:

- Target: 20%
- Relative threshold: 20% of target
- Allowed drift: 4 percentage points

This method can create narrower limits for large categories and smaller absolute limits for small categories.

## Absolute Threshold

An absolute threshold uses fixed percentage points.

Example:

- Rebalance when drift exceeds 5 percentage points

## Rebalancing Bands

A rebalancing band defines the permitted allocation range.

| Asset Category | Target | Minimum | Maximum |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cash | 10% | 8% | 15% |
| Fixed income | 40% | 35% | 45% |
| Global equities | 40% | 35% | 45% |
| Other | 10% | 5% | 15% |

The actual bands should reflect the Investment Policy Statement.

## Rebalancing Priority

A conservative priority order may be:

1. Stop purchases into overweight assets.
2. Direct new contributions to underweight assets.
3. Use dividends and interest.
4. Use scheduled withdrawals.
5. Use maturing CDTs.
6. Sell assets only when necessary.
7. Review tax and transaction effects.
8. Update the target if circumstances changed.

## Cash-Flow Rebalancing

Cash-flow rebalancing uses money entering or leaving the portfolio rather than selling.

Possible inflows include:

- Pension surplus
- salary savings
- consulting income
- dividends
- interest
- property-sale proceeds
- CDT maturity

Possible outflows include:

- retirement withdrawal
- property down payment
- medical expense
- debt payment
- tax payment

## Contribution Rebalancing

Direct new contributions toward underweight categories.

Advantages:

- May reduce taxable sales
- lowers transaction activity
- simpler execution
- naturally restores allocation

## Dividend and Interest Rebalancing

Instead of automatically reinvesting into the same asset, income may be directed toward:

- Underweight category
- cash reserve
- retirement spending
- tax reserve

Review tax and account restrictions before changing reinvestment instructions.

## Withdrawal Rebalancing

When money must be withdrawn, consider taking it from overweight assets.

This may:

- provide required cash
- reduce drift
- avoid additional trades

But review:

- tax lots
- holding period
- transaction fees
- settlement timing
- currency needs
- future income needs

## Maturity Rebalancing

A maturing CDT or bond can provide a natural rebalancing point.

Before automatic renewal:

- Review allocation
- review interest rates
- confirm liquidity needs
- confirm currency exposure
- redirect proceeds where appropriate

## Sale-Based Rebalancing

A sale may be required when:

- Drift is material
- no new contributions are expected
- withdrawals are insufficient
- risk exceeds the approved limit
- one holding becomes excessively concentrated
- investment thesis changes

## Tax-Aware Rebalancing

Tax-aware rebalancing considers:

- Realized gains
- realized losses
- holding period
- withholding
- account type
- cost-basis method
- foreign tax treatment
- cross-border reporting
- professional preparation cost

Tax efficiency should not justify remaining outside acceptable risk limits indefinitely.

## Unrealized Gain

An unrealized gain exists when current value exceeds cost basis but the asset has not been sold.

It may still affect:

- allocation
- concentration
- risk
- estate planning
- tax planning

## Realized Gain

A realized gain generally occurs after a taxable disposition.

Record:

- Sale date
- quantity
- proceeds
- fees
- cost basis
- currency
- gain or loss
- withholding
- tax review status

## Tax-Lot Selection

Where permitted, specific-lot selection may help manage:

- Gain size
- loss realization
- holding period
- cost basis
- tax exposure

Confirm the broker election deadline and applicable tax rules.

## Loss Harvesting

Loss harvesting involves realizing a loss that may have tax value.

Risks include:

- Repurchase restrictions
- loss of market exposure
- transaction costs
- currency effects
- cross-border mismatch
- product-substitution risk
- incorrect tax assumptions

Do not use loss harvesting without professional confirmation where material.

## Tax-Loss Benefit Versus Investment Risk

A tax benefit should not be evaluated alone.

Compare:

- Expected tax benefit
- transaction cost
- spread
- time out of market
- replacement-product risk
- reporting complexity
- professional fees

## Tax Reserve

After a taxable sale, set aside an appropriate tax reserve based on qualified advice.

Do not reinvest all sale proceeds when part may be owed in tax.

## Transaction-Cost Review

Rebalancing costs may include:

- Broker commission
- bid-ask spread
- currency conversion
- wire fee
- transfer fee
- taxes
- withholding
- professional advice
- reporting cost

## Minimum Trade Size

Small rebalancing trades may be inefficient.

Define a minimum trade size based on:

- Fixed fees
- spread
- account minimum
- operational burden
- tax-lot complexity

## Rebalancing Benefit

Approximate rebalancing benefit should be evaluated against:

- Risk reduction
- concentration reduction
- liquidity improvement
- transaction cost
- tax cost
- complexity

## Portfolio Drift Score

Possible classifications:

### Green

- All categories within permitted bands
- no material concentration
- liquidity adequate
- no urgent action

### Yellow

- One or more categories near limits
- cash flow can correct drift
- review required

### Red

- Category outside maximum or minimum
- concentration exceeds policy
- retirement liquidity affected
- material currency mismatch
- immediate decision gate required

## Concentration Controls

Review concentration by:

- Asset class
- security
- issuer
- broker
- bank
- country
- currency
- property
- sector
- income source

## Single-Security Limit

A single fund may still contain diversified holdings, but concentration can arise through:

- Narrow sector
- one country
- leverage
- one issuer
- specialized strategy
- overlapping funds

## Overlap

Fund overlap occurs when multiple ETFs hold many of the same securities.

This can create hidden concentration.

Review:

- Top holdings
- sector allocation
- country allocation
- index methodology
- market-cap exposure

## Property and Portfolio Rebalancing

A property purchase can materially change household allocation.

Before purchase, calculate:

- Property percentage of net worth
- property percentage of investable assets
- liquid assets remaining
- debt exposure
- currency exposure

After purchase, the liquid portfolio may need less real-estate-related exposure.

## Property Sale Proceeds

Property-sale proceeds should not be reinvested immediately without:

- Loan payoff
- tax reserve
- emergency reserve restoration
- near-term spending review
- allocation review
- currency plan
- cooling-off period

## Currency Rebalancing

Currency allocation may drift because of:

- Exchange-rate movement
- pension receipts
- property expenses
- foreign investments
- conversions
- travel
- debt

Review assets and obligations in:

- COP
- USD
- other currencies

## Currency Target

A currency target should support:

- Near-term expenses
- debt payments
- emergency reserves
- long-term diversification
- estate needs

Do not rebalance solely based on a short-term exchange-rate forecast.

## Rebalancing During Market Decline

During a decline:

- Confirm emergency liquidity
- confirm allocation
- review threshold
- avoid panic
- verify thesis
- use written rules
- rebalance gradually where appropriate
- preserve tax and cash-flow records

## Rebalancing During Market Gains

During strong gains:

- Measure drift
- review concentration
- review tax impact
- avoid assuming gains will continue
- use contributions and withdrawals
- restore risk limits where required

## Rebalancing and Retirement Withdrawals

Retirement withdrawals can be integrated with rebalancing.

Possible process:

1. Determine annual cash need.
2. Use pension and reliable income first.
3. Use cash reserve.
4. Refill cash from overweight assets.
5. review tax lots.
6. preserve target allocation.
7. avoid forced sales during severe declines where possible.

## Cash Reserve Refill

Define a rule for restoring retirement cash.

Possible triggers:

- Annual review
- Cash below twelve months of planned withdrawals
- Asset category above maximum band
- CDT maturity
- Strong market gains

## Sequence Risk

Large withdrawals during market declines can permanently reduce recovery potential.

Controls may include:

- Cash bucket
- fixed-income reserve
- flexible discretionary spending
- withdrawal reduction
- diversified income
- delayed large purchases

## Rebalancing After Life Changes

Review allocation after:

- Retirement
- new employment
- job loss
- marriage
- new dependent
- death
- medical diagnosis
- inheritance
- property purchase
- property sale
- relocation
- major debt change

The correct action may be to revise the target rather than trade back to an outdated target.

## Target-Allocation Change

A target should change only after documented review.

Record:

- Reason
- previous target
- new target
- risk impact
- tax impact
- liquidity impact
- professional review
- effective date

Do not change the target merely to avoid admitting drift.

## Rebalancing Decision Process

1. Calculate current values.
2. calculate current percentages.
3. compare with target bands.
4. identify concentration.
5. review liquidity.
6. review contributions and withdrawals.
7. review taxes and fees.
8. select lowest-cost correction.
9. complete decision gate.
10. execute and reconcile.
11. update records.

## Rebalancing Calculation

Target value equals:

Total portfolio value
multiplied by target percentage

Required adjustment equals:

Target value
minus current value

A positive result indicates an underweight category.

A negative result indicates an overweight category.

## Example

Total portfolio:

COP 500 million

Target equity:

40%

Target equity value:

COP 200 million

Current equity value:

COP 245 million

Required adjustment:

COP 200 million
minus COP 245 million
equals negative COP 45 million

Equity is overweight by COP 45 million relative to the exact target.

A trade may not be required if the position remains inside the permitted band.

## Rebalancing Tolerance

Exact target matching is usually unnecessary.

Reasons include:

- Prices change continuously
- fees
- taxes
- settlement
- rounding
- fractional-share limits
- property valuation uncertainty

The goal is acceptable risk control, not mathematical perfection.

## Data Quality

Portfolio calculations require current and accurate:

- Market values
- exchange rates
- property estimates
- loan balances
- cash balances
- account statements
- settlement status

Do not rebalance using stale or inconsistent valuations.

## Property Valuation Frequency

Illiquid property does not require daily valuation.

Possible review frequency:

- Annually
- after major renovation
- after material market change
- before financing
- before sale
- before major allocation decision

Use conservative value ranges.

## Open Orders and Unsettled Trades

Before rebalancing:

- Review open orders
- review unsettled trades
- confirm available cash
- avoid duplicate transactions
- account for pending dividends
- account for maturing deposits

## Rebalancing Execution Plan

For each trade, record:

- Account
- security
- buy or sell
- quantity
- estimated value
- order type
- tax lot
- expected fees
- expected settlement
- target allocation after trade

## Partial Rebalancing

A full return to target may be unnecessary.

Partial rebalancing may:

- Move the portfolio inside permitted bands
- reduce tax cost
- reduce market impact
- preserve liquidity
- spread execution over time

## Staged Rebalancing

A staged plan can divide trades across:

- Dates
- contributions
- maturities
- tax years
- market sessions

Staging should follow written rules rather than emotional market timing.

## Automatic Investment Plans

Review automatic purchases when:

- Asset becomes overweight
- income changes
- emergency reserve declines
- target allocation changes
- product changes
- fees change
- tax status changes

## Rebalancing and Debt

Debt reduction can be part of allocation planning.

An extra debt payment may function as a low-risk use of capital.

Compare:

- Interest avoided
- liquidity lost
- investment allocation
- currency
- tax treatment
- reserve level

## Rebalancing Stop Conditions

Do not execute when:

- Target allocation is not documented
- values are stale
- tax impact is unknown
- liquidity gate is yellow or red
- funds include emergency reserves
- account instructions are inconsistent
- open orders are unresolved
- cost basis is missing
- property transaction is incomplete
- decision is driven by market fear or excitement
- transaction costs exceed reasonable benefit
- professional review is required but incomplete

## Monthly Monitoring

Monthly review may include:

- Cash
- major portfolio values
- contributions
- withdrawals
- drift alerts
- concentration
- open orders
- upcoming maturities

Trading is not necessarily required monthly.

## Quarterly Review

Quarterly review may include:

- Full allocation
- currency exposure
- institution exposure
- property concentration
- threshold breaches
- planned cash flows
- tax estimates
- contribution direction

## Annual Rebalancing Review

Annual review should include:

- Investment Policy Statement
- target allocation
- actual allocation
- permitted bands
- life changes
- retirement needs
- property exposure
- debt
- taxes
- fees
- realized gains and losses
- performance
- required policy changes

## Rebalancing Documentation

Retain:

- Valuation snapshot
- target allocation
- drift calculation
- decision rationale
- tax review
- transaction plan
- trade confirmations
- settlement records
- post-trade allocation
- reconciliation

## Beginner Rules

1. Rebalance to manage risk, not predict markets.
2. Use the household allocation, not one account alone.
3. Prefer contributions, withdrawals, and maturities before taxable sales.
4. Use written thresholds.
5. Include property and currency exposure.
6. Review fees and taxes before selling.
7. Do not pursue exact percentages unnecessarily.
8. Update targets only after documented review.
9. Reconcile every rebalancing transaction.
10. Stop when liquidity, tax, or data quality is unresolved.
