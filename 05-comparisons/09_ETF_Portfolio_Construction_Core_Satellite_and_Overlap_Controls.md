# Lesson 60 — ETF Portfolio Construction, Core-Satellite Allocation, Factor Tilts, and Overlap Controls

## Learning Objective

Learn how to construct an ETF portfolio by assigning each fund a defined role, establishing target allocations and limits, controlling overlap, evaluating factor and currency exposure, measuring portfolio-level risk, and preventing unnecessary product complexity.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

Asset allocation, product suitability, tax treatment, estate exposure, investor eligibility, and expected returns depend on individual circumstances and can change.

Use current official fund documentation and qualified financial, tax, and legal professionals for material decisions.

## Core Principle

A collection of individually acceptable ETFs is not automatically a coherent portfolio.

Every ETF should have:

- A defined purpose
- target allocation
- minimum and maximum allocation
- approved benchmark
- expected source of return
- known risk contribution
- overlap analysis
- currency role
- tax and estate review
- monitoring rule
- exit trigger

## Portfolio Construction

Portfolio construction is the process of combining assets so the overall portfolio supports:

- Financial goals
- time horizon
- required liquidity
- acceptable loss
- withdrawal needs
- currency obligations
- diversification
- tax efficiency
- operational simplicity

## Portfolio Objective

A portfolio objective should state:

- What the money is for
- when it may be needed
- required level of certainty
- target currency
- acceptable drawdown
- required liquidity
- expected contribution or withdrawal pattern

## Goal-Based Portfolio

Different goals may require separate portfolios or allocation sleeves.

Examples:

- Emergency liquidity
- near-term vehicle purchase
- property down payment
- retirement income
- long-term growth
- medical reserve
- legacy assets

## Portfolio Sleeve

A portfolio sleeve is a defined component serving a particular role.

Possible sleeves include:

- Cash and liquidity
- high-quality fixed income
- domestic equity
- global equity
- inflation-sensitive assets
- property exposure
- satellite strategies

## Core Portfolio

The core portfolio generally provides broad, diversified, lower-complexity exposure.

Possible characteristics include:

- Broad market coverage
- transparent methodology
- low cost
- strong liquidity
- low turnover
- established fund
- simple monitoring
- clear portfolio role

## Satellite Portfolio

A satellite allocation adds limited exposure intended to:

- Emphasize a factor
- add a region
- add a sector
- pursue income
- reduce a specific risk
- express a controlled conviction

Satellite positions should not undermine the core portfolio.

## Core-Satellite Structure

A core-satellite structure may contain:

- Large core allocation
- smaller satellite allocations
- strict position limits
- explicit overlap review
- defined review dates
- removal rules

## Core Allocation

A core allocation should normally dominate the portfolio’s:

- Capital
- diversification
- benchmark exposure
- long-term return behavior

## Satellite Limit

Set a maximum combined satellite allocation.

The limit should reflect:

- Knowledge
- risk tolerance
- monitoring capacity
- tax complexity
- account size
- goal importance

## Portfolio Role

Possible ETF roles include:

- Broad global equity
- domestic equity
- emerging-market equity
- small-cap equity
- value tilt
- quality tilt
- low-volatility tilt
- dividend income
- government bonds
- investment-grade bonds
- inflation-linked bonds
- short-duration reserves
- real-estate exposure
- commodity exposure

## Role Duplication

Role duplication occurs when several ETFs perform substantially the same function.

Examples:

- Two broad United States equity funds
- several global equity funds with similar holdings
- technology ETF plus broad index already dominated by technology
- dividend ETF plus value ETF holding the same companies

## Portfolio Complexity

Portfolio complexity increases with:

- More funds
- more accounts
- more currencies
- more tax regimes
- more rebalancing bands
- more benchmarks
- more derivatives
- more professional-review requirements

Complexity should provide a measurable benefit.

## Complexity Test

For every additional ETF, ask:

1. What new exposure does it provide?
2. Which goal does it support?
3. How is it different from existing holdings?
4. What risk does it add?
5. What cost does it add?
6. What monitoring does it require?
7. What simpler alternative exists?

## Redundant ETF

An ETF may be redundant when it:

- Duplicates existing holdings
- does not change portfolio behavior materially
- adds administrative cost
- increases tax complexity
- creates negligible diversification benefit

## Target Allocation

Target allocation is the intended percentage assigned to each asset class, sleeve, or ETF.

## Allocation Band

An allocation band defines acceptable movement around the target.

Example:

Target:

20%

Minimum:

15%

Maximum:

25%

## Hard Limit

A hard limit must not be exceeded without documented approval.

## Soft Limit

A soft limit triggers review but may permit temporary deviation.

## Strategic Asset Allocation

Strategic allocation is the long-term target based on goals and risk capacity.

## Tactical Allocation

Tactical allocation temporarily deviates from the strategic target based on a market view.

Tactical changes introduce:

- Timing risk
- forecasting risk
- transaction cost
- tax
- behavioral risk

A beginner portfolio may prohibit tactical allocation.

## Policy Benchmark

A policy benchmark combines indexes according to the strategic allocation.

Example:

60% global equity index
plus
40% bond index

## Blended Benchmark Return

Blended benchmark return equals:

Sum of:

Asset-class benchmark weight
multiplied by
benchmark return

## Benchmark Rebalancing

The policy benchmark should use a documented rebalancing method.

Possible methods include:

- Monthly
- quarterly
- annually
- never within the reporting period

## Asset Allocation Versus Product Selection

Asset allocation decides how much belongs in:

- Equity
- bonds
- cash
- other assets

Product selection decides which ETF implements each allocation.

Do not begin with a fund ticker before defining the desired exposure.

## Capital Allocation Hierarchy

A controlled hierarchy may be:

1. Protect essential liquidity.
2. define financial goal.
3. determine time horizon.
4. determine acceptable loss.
5. establish strategic asset allocation.
6. select benchmark.
7. select implementation vehicle.
8. establish allocation bands.
9. approve execution.
10. monitor and rebalance.

## Equity Allocation

Equity allocation may support long-term growth but can experience substantial drawdowns.

Review:

- Goal horizon
- withdrawal timing
- concentration
- country exposure
- currency
- valuation
- household loss capacity

## Fixed-Income Allocation

Fixed income may support:

- Liquidity
- capital stability
- income
- diversification
- liability matching

Review:

- Duration
- credit quality
- currency
- maturity
- inflation
- liquidity
- reinvestment risk

## Cash Allocation

Cash may support:

- Emergency reserve
- upcoming obligations
- settlement
- rebalancing
- operating buffer

Cash should not be counted simultaneously as:

- Emergency reserve
- property down payment
- investment opportunity fund

## Risk Asset

A risk asset may include assets whose market value can decline materially.

Examples:

- Equities
- high-yield bonds
- long-duration bonds
- emerging-market debt
- commodities
- property funds

## Defensive Asset

A defensive asset is intended to reduce portfolio loss or provide liquidity.

Its behavior is not guaranteed during every crisis.

## Growth Asset

A growth asset is intended primarily to increase long-term capital.

## Income Asset

An income asset is intended to produce cash flow.

High distribution yield does not guarantee:

- Stable income
- capital preservation
- high total return

## Factor

A factor is a systematic characteristic associated with patterns of risk and return.

Common equity factors include:

- Market
- size
- value
- momentum
- quality
- low volatility
- profitability
- investment

## Factor Tilt

A factor tilt increases exposure to a chosen factor relative to a broad benchmark.

## Factor Premium

A factor premium is the historical excess return associated with a factor under a specified methodology.

A historical premium may:

- Disappear
- reverse
- remain negative for long periods
- be reduced by cost
- be difficult to capture

## Factor Methodology Risk

Different indexes may define the same factor differently.

Example value measures may use:

- Price-to-book
- price-to-earnings
- cash flow
- sales
- dividends
- composite scoring

## Factor Crowding

Factor crowding may occur when many investors pursue similar exposure.

Possible effects include:

- Higher valuations
- reduced future return
- rapid reversals
- liquidity stress

## Factor Cycles

Factors may underperform for extended periods.

A factor tilt requires:

- Long horizon
- discipline
- understanding
- limited allocation
- rebalancing rule

## Value Tilt

A value tilt emphasizes securities considered inexpensive relative to selected fundamentals.

Risks include:

- Value traps
- sector concentration
- structural decline
- prolonged underperformance

## Small-Cap Tilt

A small-cap tilt emphasizes smaller companies.

Risks include:

- Higher volatility
- lower liquidity
- weaker profitability
- greater economic sensitivity
- higher transaction cost

## Quality Tilt

A quality tilt may emphasize:

- Profitability
- balance-sheet strength
- earnings stability
- capital efficiency

Definitions vary.

## Momentum Tilt

A momentum tilt emphasizes recent relative performance.

Risks include:

- High turnover
- sharp reversal
- trading cost
- tax realization

## Low-Volatility Tilt

A low-volatility tilt emphasizes securities with lower historical variability.

Risks include:

- Sector concentration
- valuation
- interest-rate sensitivity
- failure during unusual regimes

## Dividend Tilt

A dividend tilt emphasizes companies paying distributions.

Risks include:

- Yield traps
- sector concentration
- dividend cuts
- tax drag
- lower diversification

## Multi-Factor ETF

A multi-factor ETF combines several factors.

Review:

- Factor definitions
- weights
- interaction
- turnover
- constraints
- historical backtest
- live performance

## Factor Overlap

Two factor ETFs may hold many of the same securities.

A portfolio can unintentionally create:

- Large value exposure
- excessive small-cap exposure
- sector concentration
- duplicated positions

## Factor Budget

A factor budget limits the total intentional deviation from the broad market.

## Active Share

Active share measures how different a portfolio is from a benchmark based on holdings weights.

A low active share may indicate that a satellite adds little distinct exposure.

## Active Weight

Active weight equals:

Portfolio weight
minus benchmark weight

## Positive Active Weight

A positive active weight is an overweight relative to the benchmark.

## Negative Active Weight

A negative active weight is an underweight.

## Tracking Error Budget

A tracking-error budget limits how much portfolio behavior may differ from the policy benchmark.

## Portfolio Overlap

Portfolio overlap should be reviewed at:

- Security level
- issuer level
- sector level
- country level
- currency level
- factor level

## Look-Through Exposure

Look-through analysis examines the underlying holdings of each ETF rather than treating every ETF as a separate asset.

## Combined Security Weight

Combined security weight equals:

Sum across funds of:

ETF portfolio weight
multiplied by
security weight within ETF

## Example

ETF A is 50% of the portfolio and holds Company X at 8%.

ETF B is 20% of the portfolio and holds Company X at 5%.

Combined Company X exposure equals:

50% × 8%
plus
20% × 5%

Result:

5%

## Combined Sector Weight

Combined sector weight is calculated in the same way using sector weights.

## Combined Country Weight

Combined country weight is calculated using country exposures across funds.

## Fund-of-Funds Exposure

An ETF may hold other funds.

Look-through analysis may require additional layers.

## Double Counting

Do not count:

- ETF shares
- and underlying holdings

as separate economic assets in the same exposure total.

## Portfolio Concentration

Portfolio concentration may be hidden even when each ETF appears diversified.

## Single-Issuer Limit

Set a maximum combined exposure to one issuer across all funds.

## Single-Sector Limit

Set a maximum combined exposure to one sector.

## Single-Country Limit

Set a maximum combined exposure to one country where appropriate.

## Currency Limit

Set a maximum unhedged exposure to one currency based on:

- Goal currency
- income
- expenses
- debt
- property
- investment policy

## Home Bias

Home bias is excessive exposure to the investor’s local market.

A local allocation may still be appropriate for:

- Local obligations
- regulatory familiarity
- currency matching

The allocation should be intentional.

## Foreign Bias

Foreign bias may occur when nearly all investments depend on foreign markets or currencies while expenses are local.

## Currency Look-Through

Trading currency does not determine economic currency exposure.

Review:

- Underlying companies
- bond denomination
- hedge policy
- portfolio revenue
- liability currency

## Regional Allocation

Possible regional categories include:

- Colombia
- Latin America
- United States
- developed markets
- emerging markets
- global markets

Provider classifications may differ.

## Emerging-Market Allocation

Emerging-market exposure may add diversification and growth potential but can involve:

- Political risk
- currency risk
- governance risk
- liquidity
- capital controls
- concentration

## Portfolio Risk Contribution

Capital weight is not the same as risk contribution.

A volatile ETF may contribute more portfolio risk than its allocation suggests.

## Approximate Risk Contribution

Risk contribution depends on:

- Portfolio weight
- volatility
- correlation
- covariance

## Risk Concentration

Risk concentration occurs when one position or factor drives a disproportionate share of portfolio volatility or drawdown.

## Marginal Risk

Marginal risk estimates how portfolio risk changes when an allocation changes.

## Diversification Ratio

A diversification ratio may compare:

Weighted average individual volatility
with
portfolio volatility

Its usefulness depends on data quality and assumptions.

## Expected Return Assumption

Expected returns should be:

- Presented as ranges
- linked to sources
- separated from historical returns
- reduced for fees and tax where appropriate
- reviewed periodically

## Capital-Market Assumption

A capital-market assumption estimates future:

- Return
- volatility
- correlation
- inflation
- interest rates

It is uncertain and should not be presented as fact.

## Optimization

Portfolio optimization selects weights according to a mathematical objective.

Possible objectives include:

- Maximize expected return for risk
- minimize volatility
- maximize risk-adjusted return
- match liabilities
- minimize tracking error

## Optimization Risk

Optimization can be highly sensitive to small changes in:

- Expected return
- volatility
- correlation
- constraints

## Estimation Error

Expected return is especially difficult to estimate precisely.

Optimization may produce unstable or extreme allocations.

## Constraint

Useful constraints may include:

- Minimum cash
- maximum equity
- maximum single fund
- maximum country
- maximum satellite allocation
- no leverage
- no complex product
- minimum liquidity

## Naive Diversification

Simple equal weighting may sometimes be more robust than highly optimized allocations, but it may still create inappropriate risk.

## Resampled or Robust Approach

More advanced methods attempt to reduce sensitivity to estimation error.

They still depend on assumptions.

## Portfolio Simplicity Rule

Prefer the simplest portfolio that:

- Meets the goal
- remains within risk capacity
- provides sufficient diversification
- supports currency needs
- can be monitored correctly

## Number of Funds

There is no universally correct number of ETFs.

More funds may increase:

- Overlap
- rebalancing work
- tax lots
- fees
- monitoring
- execution errors

## One-Fund Portfolio

A diversified all-in-one fund may simplify:

- Allocation
- rebalancing
- monitoring

Review:

- Domicile

- cost
- allocation
- currency
- estate exposure
- ability to customize

## Two-Fund Portfolio

A simple portfolio may combine:

- Broad equity
- high-quality fixed income

## Three-Fund Portfolio

A three-fund approach may separate:

- Domestic equity

## Custom Multi-Fund Portfolio

A custom portfolio provides more control but increases:

- Complexity

- tax administration

## Contribution Allocation

New contributions can be directed toward underweight assets.

This may reduce:

- Sales
- tax realization
- transaction costs

## Withdrawal Allocation

Withdrawals can come from overweight assets where consistent with:

- Tax-lot strategy
- liquidity
- goal
- policy
- settlement
- currency

## Cash-Flow Rebalancing

Cash-flow rebalancing uses:

- Contributions
- dividends
- interest
- withdrawals

to move the portfolio toward target.

## Threshold Rebalancing

Threshold rebalancing occurs when allocation crosses a defined band.

## Calendar Rebalancing

Calendar rebalancing occurs on scheduled dates.

## Hybrid Rebalancing

Hybrid rebalancing combines:

- Scheduled reviews
- threshold-triggered transactions

## Rebalancing Priority

A possible priority is:

1. Use new contributions.
2. use cash distributions.
3. adjust withdrawals.
4. sell overweight positions only when needed.

## Tax-Aware Rebalancing

Tax-aware rebalancing considers:

- Realized gain
- realized loss
- tax lot
- withholding
- account type
- reporting jurisdiction

## Rebalancing Cost

Include:

- Spread
- commission
- currency conversion
- tax
- market impact
- professional review

## Rebalancing Benefit

The benefit may include:

- Restored risk level
- reduced concentration
- disciplined selling of relative winners
- increased exposure to underweights

## Rebalancing Tolerance

Small deviations may not justify transaction costs.

## Portfolio Drift

Portfolio drift may result from:

- Market performance
- contributions
- withdrawals
- distributions
- currency movement
- benchmark changes

## Strategic Drift

Strategic drift occurs when actual portfolio design no longer matches the approved strategy.

## Product Drift

Product drift occurs when a fund’s holdings or methodology change.

## Goal Drift

Goal drift occurs when the purpose, date, or funding requirement changes.

## Portfolio Review Hierarchy

Review in this order:

1. Has the financial goal changed?
2. has loss capacity changed?
3. has the strategic allocation changed?
4. has an ETF changed?
5. has portfolio drift exceeded limits?
6. is a transaction required?

## Portfolio Implementation Plan

A portfolio implementation plan should identify:

- Target allocation
- selected ETFs
- accounts
- currencies
- order sequence
- contribution schedule
- rebalancing rules
- tax conditions
- monitoring

## Implementation Risk

Implementation risk includes:

- Wrong fund
- wrong account
- duplicate exposure
- excessive spread
- currency conversion
- unsettled cash
- tax-lot error
- partial execution

## Transition Portfolio

A transition portfolio is the temporary allocation while moving from:

- Cash to investments
- old funds to new funds
- one broker to another
- accumulation to withdrawal
- one country to another

## Staged Investment

Staged investment divides the implementation across dates.

Possible benefits:

- Reduced single-date emotional risk
- operational testing
- controlled transfer

Possible disadvantages:

- Extended cash exposure
- additional trades
- possible higher cost

## Lump-Sum Investment

Lump-sum investment deploys available capital at once.

It creates immediate target exposure but concentrates timing.

## Market-Timing Warning

Staging should follow a documented implementation rule rather than repeated delay based on fear or forecasts.

- Currency

## Near-Term Liability Sleeve

Money needed soon should generally not depend on high-volatility assets.

## Long-Term Growth Sleeve

Long-term funds may accept more volatility where:

- Withdrawals are not imminent
- reserves are adequate
- loss capacity is sufficient

## Portfolio Income

Portfolio income may come from:

- Dividends
- interest
- distributions
- planned sales

## Total-Return Withdrawal

A total-return approach funds withdrawals from:

- Income
- rebalancing
- planned asset sales

It does not require every holding to produce a high distribution.

## Yield-Chasing Risk

Yield chasing can create:

- Credit risk
- concentration
- option exposure
- return of capital
- tax drag

## Portfolio Tax Location

Tax location considers which assets belong in which account type.

Rules depend on jurisdiction and require professional review.

## Asset Location

Asset location is different from asset allocation.

Asset allocation decides what the household owns.

Asset location decides which account holds it.

## Account-Level Allocation

Each account may differ from the overall portfolio.

Evaluate both:

- Account allocation
- consolidated household allocation

## Consolidated Portfolio

A consolidated portfolio includes all investment accounts and avoids treating each account as an isolated plan.

## Household Look-Through

Household look-through may include:

- Individual accounts
- spouse accounts
- retirement accounts
- business investments
- property funds
- pensions where relevant

## Ownership and Authority

Consolidated analysis does not change legal ownership.

## Portfolio Status

### Green

- Every ETF has a defined role
- allocation is within bands
- overlap is controlled
- risk is within capacity
- liquidity is adequate
- complexity remains manageable

### Yellow

- Allocation near limit
- overlap increased
- factor tilt expanded
- currency mismatch exists
- rebalancing review required
- monitoring burden increased

### Red

- Essential reserve invested
- allocation exceeds hard limits
- portfolio role cannot be explained
- overlap creates excessive concentration
- risk exceeds household capacity
- complex product is not understood
- tax or estate treatment is unresolved

## Automatic Stop Conditions

Do not approve a portfolio implementation or change when:

- Financial goal is undefined
- emergency reserve is incomplete
- target allocation is not approved
- an ETF lacks a defined role
- combined exposure cannot be calculated
- overlap analysis is incomplete
- hard concentration limits would be exceeded
- currency needs are ignored
- risk exceeds loss capacity
- tax or estate review is materially incomplete
- satellite allocation exceeds policy
- complexity cannot be monitored
- unsettled or unavailable cash is assumed
- transaction is driven by fear of missing out

## Beginner Rules

1. Define asset allocation before selecting ETFs.
2. give every ETF one clear portfolio role.
3. use broad, simple funds for the core.
4. limit satellite and factor tilts.
5. calculate look-through exposure.
6. control combined issuer, sector, country, and currency concentration.
7. use allocation bands and documented rebalancing rules.
8. manage the household as one consolidated portfolio.
9. compare added complexity with measurable benefit.
10. stop when the portfolio cannot be clearly explained.
