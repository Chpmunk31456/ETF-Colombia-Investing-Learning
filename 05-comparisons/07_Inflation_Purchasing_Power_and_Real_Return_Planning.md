# Lesson 48 — Inflation, Purchasing Power, and Real-Return Planning

## Learning Objective

Learn how inflation changes future costs, reduces purchasing power, affects investment returns, interacts with taxes and fees, creates different risks across household expense categories, and influences retirement, property, currency, and portfolio decisions.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

Inflation rates, tax rules, product terms, pension adjustments, rent rules, interest rates, and economic conditions change over time.

Use current official data and qualified professional advice before making a material financial, investment, tax, retirement, or property decision.

## Core Principle

A higher account balance does not necessarily mean greater purchasing power.

The relevant question is not only:

- How much did the investment grow?

It is also:

- How much can the resulting money buy?

## Inflation

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level over time.

As prices rise, each unit of currency generally purchases fewer goods and services.

## Purchasing Power

Purchasing power is the amount of goods and services that a specified amount of money can buy.

If prices rise faster than income or investment value, purchasing power declines.

## Deflation

Deflation is a sustained decline in the general price level.

Deflation may increase the purchasing power of cash but can also be associated with:

- Falling wages
- weaker demand
- debt stress
- declining asset values
- economic contraction

## Disinflation

Disinflation means inflation remains positive but slows.

Example:

- Inflation falls from 8% to 4%.

Prices are still rising, but at a slower rate.

## Nominal Amount

A nominal amount is stated in current currency without adjusting for inflation.

Examples:

- Nominal salary
- nominal pension
- nominal investment return
- nominal property price
- nominal interest rate

## Real Amount

A real amount adjusts for changes in purchasing power.

Real values help compare economic outcomes across different periods.

## Nominal Return

Nominal return is the investment return before adjusting for inflation.

Example:

- Portfolio return: 7%
- Inflation: 5%

The exact real return is less than 2%.

## Exact Real-Return Formula

Real return equals:

(1 + nominal return)
divided by
(1 + inflation rate)
minus 1

Example:

Nominal return:

7%

Inflation:

5%

Real return:

(1.07 ÷ 1.05) - 1

Approximate real return:

1.90%

## Approximate Real Return

A simplified approximation is:

Nominal return
minus inflation

Using the same example:

7% minus 5% equals approximately 2%.

The approximation becomes less accurate when rates are high.

## Negative Real Return

A negative real return occurs when investment growth is insufficient to preserve purchasing power.

Example:

- Savings account earns 3%
- inflation is 6%

The nominal balance grows, but real purchasing power declines.

## After-Fee Real Return

After-fee real return should account for:

- Fund expenses
- trading costs
- advisory fees
- custody charges
- currency costs
- account fees

Educational sequence:

1. Calculate return after fees.
2. adjust the after-fee return for inflation.

## After-Tax Real Return

After-tax real return also considers applicable tax effects.

Possible sources of tax drag include:

- Interest income
- dividends
- realized gains
- withholding
- property income
- foreign reporting costs

Tax treatment requires jurisdiction-specific professional review.

## Full Real-Return Analysis

A practical real-return analysis may compare:

Gross nominal return
minus fees
minus taxes
adjusted for inflation

This should be calculated consistently rather than mixing before-tax and after-tax values.

## Future Cost

A future-cost estimate projects the amount required to purchase something later.

Future cost equals:

Current cost
multiplied by
(1 + annual cost increase)
raised to the number of years

## Example — Future Household Expense

Current annual expense:

COP 60 million

Assumed annual increase:

5%

Time:

10 years

Future annual expense:

COP 60 million × (1.05)^10

Approximate future annual expense:

COP 97.73 million

## Compounding Inflation

Inflation compounds.

A 5% increase each year does not produce only a 50% increase over ten years.

Because each increase applies to a growing base, the cumulative increase is greater.

## Purchasing-Power Decline

Future purchasing power of current money may be estimated as:

Current amount
divided by
(1 + inflation)
raised to the number of years

## Example — Cash Purchasing Power

Current cash:

COP 100 million

Inflation assumption:

5%

Time:

10 years

Purchasing power in current-money terms:

COP 100 million ÷ (1.05)^10

Approximate result:

COP 61.39 million

The account may still show COP 100 million, but its real purchasing power is substantially lower.

## Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 provides a rough estimate of how long prices may take to double.

Approximate doubling time equals:

72
divided by annual inflation rate

Example:

At 6% annual inflation:

72 ÷ 6 = approximately 12 years

This is only an approximation.

## General Inflation Versus Personal Inflation

Published inflation measures reflect a broad basket of goods and services.

A household’s personal inflation may differ because of spending on:

- Housing
- health care
- medication
- food
- transportation
- education
- travel
- insurance
- property administration
- imported goods
- dependent support

## Personal Inflation Rate

A household can estimate personal inflation by:

1. Listing major expense categories.
2. assigning each category a spending weight.
3. calculating the change in each category.
4. multiplying each change by its weight.
5. adding the weighted results.

## Weighted Personal Inflation Formula

Personal inflation equals:

Sum of:

Expense-category weight
multiplied by
category price change

## Example

| Category | Weight | Price Change | Contribution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Housing | 35% | 6% | 2.10 percentage points |
| Food | 20% | 8% | 1.60 percentage points |
| Health | 15% | 10% | 1.50 percentage points |
| Transport | 15% | 4% | 0.60 percentage points |
| Other | 15% | 3% | 0.45 percentage points |

Estimated personal inflation:

6.25%

## Expense Weights

Expense weights should be based on actual household spending rather than assumptions where reliable records are available.

Weights should total:

100%

## Essential Versus Discretionary Inflation

Separate:

### Essential expenses

Examples:

- Housing
- health care
- food
- insurance
- transportation
- debt
- dependent support

### Discretionary expenses

Examples:

- Optional travel
- entertainment
- premium upgrades
- elective purchases

Essential-expense inflation is especially important for retirement planning.

## Inflation by Category

Different categories may rise at different rates.

Examples include:

- Medical inflation
- food inflation
- education inflation
- construction-cost inflation
- property-tax changes
- insurance-premium increases
- vehicle costs
- rent changes
- imported-goods inflation

Do not use one inflation assumption for every goal without review.

## Headline Inflation

Headline inflation generally reflects the broad published price index.

## Core Inflation

Core inflation generally excludes specified volatile categories under the relevant methodology.

Definitions vary by institution and jurisdiction.

## Inflation Index

An inflation index measures changes in the price level of a defined basket.

Before using an index, confirm:

- Publisher
- jurisdiction
- basket
- base period
- frequency
- revision policy
- intended use

## Inflation Data Source

For material analysis, record:

- Official source
- index name
- publication date
- reference period
- annual or monthly rate
- base period
- access date

## Year-Over-Year Inflation

Year-over-year inflation compares an index with the corresponding period one year earlier.

## Month-Over-Month Inflation

Month-over-month inflation compares an index with the previous month.

Do not annualize a single monthly rate without understanding the method and volatility.

## Average Annual Inflation

Average annual inflation may compare average index levels across years.

It may differ from the year-end year-over-year rate.

## Cumulative Inflation

Cumulative inflation measures total price change over several periods.

Cumulative inflation equals:

Product of each period’s inflation factor
minus 1

## Inflation Assumption Governance

Every planning assumption should record:

- Rate
- category
- source
- start date
- review date
- low scenario
- base scenario
- high scenario
- rationale

## Scenario Planning

Use multiple scenarios rather than one forecast.

Possible educational scenarios:

- Low inflation
- base inflation
- high inflation
- temporary inflation spike
- prolonged inflation
- inflation followed by recession
- currency depreciation plus domestic inflation

## Inflation Forecast Risk

Inflation forecasts can be wrong because of:

- Energy prices
- food supply
- currency movement
- fiscal policy
- monetary policy
- wages
- housing
- geopolitical events
- supply disruptions
- weather
- regulation

Do not build a fragile plan around one precise forecast.

## Inflation and Cash

Cash provides:

- Liquidity
- nominal stability
- payment readiness

Cash may lose real value when its yield is below inflation.

## Cash Reserve Objective

An emergency reserve is not intended primarily to maximize return.

Its priorities may include:

- Accessibility
- nominal stability
- correct currency
- institutional safety
- operational reliability

Inflation risk should be managed without sacrificing the reserve’s purpose.

## Inflation and Savings Accounts

Compare:

- Nominal interest
- fees
- taxes
- inflation
- withdrawal access
- institutional protection

A positive stated interest rate may still produce a negative real return.

## Inflation and CDTs

A certificate of term deposit (CDT) may provide a known nominal rate for a defined period.

Review:

- Effective rate
- tax
- fees
- term
- liquidity
- reinvestment risk
- inflation during the term
- real return

## Reinvestment Risk

Reinvestment risk occurs when a maturing investment must be reinvested at a lower rate.

During declining inflation, nominal rates may also decline.

## Inflation and Fixed-Rate Bonds

Unexpected inflation can reduce the real value of fixed future payments.

Longer maturities may have greater exposure to:

- Inflation surprises
- interest-rate changes
- market-price decline

## Inflation-Linked Instruments

Some instruments adjust principal, interest, or payments using an inflation index.

Review:

- Index used
- adjustment delay
- tax treatment
- liquidity
- issuer risk
- maturity
- real yield
- fees
- currency

An inflation-linked instrument does not eliminate every risk.

## Inflation and Equities

Equities may provide long-term growth that exceeds inflation, but results are not guaranteed.

Inflation may affect companies differently based on:

- Pricing power
- debt
- wages
- input costs
- regulation
- consumer demand
- capital intensity
- valuation

## Inflation and Property

Property may provide partial inflation exposure through:

- Rent adjustments
- replacement cost
- land scarcity
- nominal price increases

However, property also faces:

- Vacancy
- maintenance inflation
- property tax
- insurance increases
- administration fees
- financing costs
- legal limits
- illiquidity

## Property Real Return

A property real-return analysis should include:

- Net rental income
- vacancy
- administration
- tax
- insurance
- maintenance
- renovation
- financing
- selling costs
- price change
- inflation

Gross property-price appreciation alone is incomplete.

## Rental Income and Inflation

Review whether rental income can keep pace with:

- Maintenance
- administration
- insurance
- tax
- vacancy
- mortgage payments
- household expenses

Legal and contractual rules may affect rent adjustments.

## Renovation Inflation

Construction and renovation costs may rise differently from general inflation.

Track:

- Materials
- labor
- permits
- professional fees
- transportation
- imported components
- contingency

## Insurance Inflation

Insurance planning should review:

- Replacement cost
- policy limits
- deductibles
- premium increases
- construction costs
- medical costs
- underinsurance risk

A policy limit may become inadequate even when it was reasonable when issued.

## Inflation and Debt

Inflation may reduce the real burden of fixed-rate debt when:

- Income rises
- payments remain fixed
- the borrower remains solvent

Inflation may increase stress when debt has:

- Variable rate
- inflation-linked payments
- short reset period
- foreign currency
- high refinancing risk

## Inflation and Variable-Rate Debt

Variable-rate debt may become more expensive when interest rates rise in response to inflation.

Stress test:

- Higher rate
- higher monthly payment
- higher household expenses
- lower investment values
- lower income

## Inflation and Income

Income may be:

- Fully adjusted
- partly adjusted
- delayed
- fixed
- irregular
- linked to another currency

Review whether income growth keeps pace with essential-expense inflation.

## Pension Inflation

A pension may be:

- Fixed
- periodically adjusted
- linked to an index
- subject to caps
- paid in another currency
- affected by taxes and exchange rates

Verify the actual adjustment mechanism from official documents.

## Retirement Inflation Risk

Retirement plans may span decades.

Inflation can affect:

- Food
- housing
- health care
- insurance
- dependent support
- transportation
- travel
- property maintenance

Small annual differences can compound into large long-term funding gaps.

## Retirement Spending Phases

Spending may change across retirement.

Possible phases include:

- Active retirement
- slower travel period
- increased health-care needs
- assisted-living or caregiver period

Each phase may experience different inflation.

## Longevity and Inflation

Longevity increases exposure to cumulative inflation.

A plan that appears sufficient for ten years may be inadequate over twenty or thirty years.

## Sequence of Inflation Risk

Sequence of inflation risk occurs when high inflation arrives early in retirement while withdrawals are occurring.

Early inflation may permanently increase the future spending base.

## Inflation-Adjusted Withdrawal

A fixed real withdrawal method increases nominal withdrawals with inflation.

Example:

Year one withdrawal:

COP 60 million

Inflation:

5%

Year two nominal withdrawal:

COP 63 million

This method can create portfolio stress during poor market periods.

## Flexible Withdrawal

A flexible policy may adjust spending based on:

- Inflation
- portfolio performance
- essential needs
- pension income
- cash reserve
- market drawdown
- longevity risk

## Essential Spending Floor

Define the minimum annual amount required for:

- Housing
- food
- health
- insurance
- transport
- debt
- dependent support

Apply an inflation scenario to this floor.

## Inflation and Financial Goals

Each goal should use an appropriate cost-growth assumption.

Examples:

- Vehicle
- property down payment
- education
- medical treatment
- travel
- renovation
- insurance deductible
- estate cost

## Goal Funding Shortfall

A goal can become underfunded when:

- Target cost rises
- contributions remain unchanged
- investment returns are lower than assumed
- currency moves adversely
- fees increase
- deadline shortens

## Inflation-Adjusted Goal Target

Future target equals:

Current estimated cost
multiplied by
(1 + goal-specific cost increase)
raised to years remaining

## Currency and Inflation

Cross-border households may experience:

- Domestic inflation
- foreign inflation
- exchange-rate movement
- transfer fees
- conversion spreads
- different interest rates

These effects should be modeled separately.

## Reporting Currency

A portfolio may appear to grow in COP because:

- The investment increased
- USD strengthened
- COP weakened
- both occurred

Separate asset return from currency return.

## Inflation Differential

An inflation differential is the difference between inflation rates in two jurisdictions.

It may influence long-term currency relationships, but actual exchange rates can move differently for extended periods.

## Imported Inflation

Imported inflation may occur when foreign goods or inputs become more expensive because of:

- Currency depreciation
- higher global prices
- transportation costs
- tariffs
- supply restrictions

## Household Currency Match

Near-term expenses should generally be supported by assets in the expected spending currency where practical.

This reduces dependence on favorable exchange rates at the payment date.

## Real Exchange-Rate Effect

A real exchange-rate analysis considers both:

- Nominal exchange-rate change
- inflation differences

This is more complex than observing the currency quote alone.

## Inflation and Taxes

Tax systems may use nominal amounts.

This may cause taxation of gains that are partly or entirely attributable to inflation.

Professional review may be required to determine:

- Tax basis
- indexation
- nominal gain
- real gain
- withholding
- foreign exchange treatment

## Nominal Gain Versus Real Gain

Example:

- Asset price rises 10%
- inflation is 8%

The nominal gain is 10%.

The approximate real gain is much smaller.

Tax may still be based on nominal rules.

## Inflation and Fees

A fixed nominal fee may become less burdensome in real terms.

A percentage fee grows with asset value.

Review both:

- Nominal fee
- real fee burden
- percentage of assets
- percentage of income

## Inflation and Insurance Deductibles

A fixed deductible may decline in real terms.

A percentage deductible may rise with insured value.

Review whether reserves remain sufficient.

## Inflation and Emergency Reserves

Emergency-reserve targets should be reviewed when essential monthly expenses rise.

Example:

Previous essential monthly expenses:

COP 5 million

Six-month target:

COP 30 million

New essential monthly expenses:

COP 6 million

Updated six-month target:

COP 36 million

## Reserve-Recalculation Trigger

Recalculate reserves after:

- Annual expense review
- major rent change
- medical change
- insurance change
- new dependent
- vehicle purchase
- debt change
- relocation
- sustained inflation

## Inflation and Lifestyle Creep

Not every spending increase is inflation.

Lifestyle creep occurs when spending rises because of:

- Higher quality
- greater quantity
- optional upgrades
- expanded services
- new habits

Separate price change from consumption change.

## Price Effect Versus Quantity Effect

Expense increase can be decomposed into:

- Price effect
- quantity or usage effect
- quality effect
- new category effect

## Example

Food expense rises by 15%.

Possible causes:

- Prices rose 7%
- household bought more food
- higher-quality products were selected
- delivery spending increased

Do not attribute the full increase automatically to inflation.

## Inflation Budget Variance

For each category, compare:

- Prior-year cost
- expected inflation
- expected cost
- actual cost
- variance
- cause

## Inflation Stress Test

A household inflation stress test may include:

### Scenario A — Moderate inflation

- Essential expenses rise gradually
- income partly adjusts

### Scenario B — High inflation

- Food, health, and housing rise faster
- interest rates remain high

### Scenario C — Currency shock

- COP weakens
- imported costs rise
- USD assets increase in COP terms

### Scenario D — Retirement pressure

- Pension adjustment lags
- health expenses rise faster
- portfolio return is weak

### Scenario E — Property-cost shock

- Maintenance and insurance increase
- vacancy occurs
- rent adjustment is delayed

## Stress-Test Outputs

Calculate:

- Updated annual expenses
- reserve requirement
- goal funding gap
- debt-service ratio
- portfolio withdrawal rate
- real investment return
- currency shortfall
- required corrective action

## Inflation Protection Hierarchy

A possible hierarchy is:

1. Maintain accurate spending records.
2. protect emergency liquidity.
3. match near-term currency needs.
4. reduce avoidable fees.
5. avoid excessive fixed-rate cash exposure over long horizons.
6. diversify long-term assets.
7. review income-adjustment mechanisms.
8. maintain property and insurance reserves.
9. update goals and retirement assumptions.
10. use professional review for tax and legal effects.

## Inflation Hedge

An inflation hedge is an asset or strategy expected to retain value when prices rise.

No asset is a perfect hedge in every period.

Potential characteristics may include:

- Pricing power
- inflation-linked cash flow
- real assets
- short duration
- floating rate
- diversified global exposure

## Hedge Evaluation

Evaluate an alleged inflation hedge for:

- Time horizon
- volatility
- fees
- liquidity
- tax
- currency
- correlation
- valuation
- downside risk
- historical limitations

## Gold and Commodities

Commodities may respond to inflation, supply shocks, and currency changes.

They may also experience:

- High volatility
- no cash flow
- storage or fund costs
- long weak periods
- speculative pricing

Do not assume they provide a guaranteed inflation hedge.

## Property as an Inflation Hedge

Property may provide long-term inflation sensitivity but can underperform because of:

- Overpayment
- vacancy
- financing
- legal issues
- maintenance
- poor location
- weak rent growth
- high transaction costs

## Equities as an Inflation Hedge

Companies may pass costs to customers when they have pricing power.

Other companies may suffer margin compression.

Broad diversification may reduce company-specific exposure but does not guarantee positive real returns.

## Short-Duration Fixed Income

Shorter maturities may adjust to changing rates more quickly than longer maturities.

They may still produce negative real returns.

## Real-Return Target

A real-return target should be tied to:

- Financial goal
- risk tolerance
- time horizon
- taxes
- fees
- inflation assumption
- currency

## Required Nominal Return

Approximate required nominal return equals:

Real-return target
plus inflation

The exact formula is:

Required nominal return equals:

(1 + required real return)
multiplied by
(1 + inflation)
minus 1

## Example

Required real return:

3%

Inflation:

5%

Required nominal return:

1.03 × 1.05 - 1

Approximate required nominal return:

8.15%

## Real Income Growth

Real income growth equals:

(1 + income growth)
divided by
(1 + personal inflation)
minus 1

## Example

Income growth:

6%

Personal inflation:

8%

Real income growth:

(1.06 ÷ 1.08) - 1

Approximate result:

-1.85%

Income rose nominally but purchasing power declined.

## Real Debt Cost

A simplified real debt-cost estimate may compare:

Nominal interest rate
with inflation

The exact economic effect depends on:

- Tax
- payment structure
- income growth
- variable rate
- currency
- fees

## Real Property Appreciation

Real property appreciation equals:

(1 + nominal property-price change)
divided by
(1 + inflation)
minus 1

This still excludes:

- Income
- maintenance
- tax
- insurance
- financing
- transaction costs

## Confidence Classification

Use:

### High

Based on current actual household records and authoritative data.

### Moderate

Based on reasonable estimates and partial records.

### Low

Based on broad averages, stale quotes, or unsupported assumptions.

## Inflation Status

### Green

- Essential expenses measured
- reserves updated
- goals adjusted
- real returns calculated
- income broadly keeps pace

### Yellow

- Personal inflation exceeds plan
- reserve target is stale
- goal costs increased
- pension adjustment lags
- review required

### Red

- Essential expenses materially exceed income growth
- emergency reserve is inadequate
- short-term goal depends on volatile assets
- debt payment and inflation create cash-flow stress
- retirement withdrawals are unsustainable
- assumptions are unsupported

## Automatic Stop Conditions

Do not rely on an inflation analysis when:

- Inflation source is unidentified
- nominal and real values are mixed
- rates from different periods are compared
- expense weights do not total 100%
- taxes and fees are ignored where material
- currency effects are double counted
- personal inflation is presented as official inflation
- goal-specific cost inflation is missing
- pension adjustment rules are assumed
- property appreciation is treated as guaranteed
- one precise forecast drives the entire plan

## Beginner Rules

1. Distinguish nominal and real values.
2. Use the exact real-return formula for material analysis.
3. Measure personal inflation using actual spending.
4. apply different assumptions to different goals.
5. review emergency reserves as expenses rise.
6. separate inflation from lifestyle creep.
7. include fees, taxes, and currency.
8. test retirement plans under higher inflation.
9. do not assume one asset is a perfect inflation hedge.
10. update inflation assumptions regularly.
