# Lesson 10 — Distressed Property Deal Analysis

## Learning Objective

Evaluate whether a distressed property is financially attractive after including acquisition cost, repairs, legal costs, carrying costs, financing, taxes, vacancy, and resale risk.

## Core Principle

The winning bid is only one part of the investment.

A property should be evaluated using:

- Total cash required
- Time to possession
- Time to renovation
- Expected rent
- Expected resale value
- Financing cost
- Taxes and fees
- Worst-case delay
- Alternative uses of the capital

## Total Project Cost

Calculate:

Total project cost =
Winning bid
+ taxes and registration
+ legal costs
+ possession costs
+ unpaid obligations
+ repairs
+ contingency
+ financing costs
+ carrying costs

## Carrying Costs

Possible carrying costs include:

- Administration fee
- Property tax
- Utilities
- Insurance
- Security
- Interest
- Maintenance
- Property management
- Vacancy
- Legal follow-up

Estimate carrying costs for at least:

- 6 months
- 12 months
- 18 months

## Repair Contingency

Use a contingency because distressed properties often contain unknown conditions.

Suggested educational scenarios:

- Full inspection available: 10% to 15%
- Limited inspection: 20% to 30%
- No interior inspection: 30% or more

The correct percentage depends on the property and professional estimates.

## Rental Scenario

Calculate:

Annual gross rent =
Monthly rent × 12

Annual net operating income =
Annual gross rent
minus vacancy
minus administration
minus property tax
minus insurance
minus maintenance
minus management
minus recurring legal or compliance costs

## Capitalization Rate

Capitalization rate estimates the property's unleveraged income return.

Capitalization rate =
Annual net operating income
divided by total project cost

A higher capitalization rate is not automatically better if:

- The property is in a risky area
- Vacancy is high
- Repairs are underestimated
- Rent is uncertain
- Title or possession is unresolved

## Cash-on-Cash Return

Cash-on-cash return compares annual pre-tax cash flow with actual cash invested.

Cash-on-cash return =
Annual pre-tax cash flow
divided by total cash invested

This is useful when financing is involved.

## Resale Scenario

Calculate:

Net resale proceeds =
Expected sale price
minus selling commission
minus taxes
minus legal costs
minus remaining debt
minus closing costs

Estimated profit =
Net resale proceeds
minus total project cost

## Margin of Safety

A distressed property should have a meaningful margin of safety because assumptions may be wrong.

Possible risks include:

- Lower resale price
- Higher repairs
- Longer possession process
- Higher legal fees
- Lower rent
- Longer vacancy
- Additional taxes
- Financing delays
- Building assessments

## Three Scenarios

### Best case

Assume:

- Fast possession
- Repairs at low estimate
- Strong rent or resale
- No major title problem

### Base case

Assume:

- Moderate delay
- Repairs at expected estimate
- Normal vacancy
- Normal selling time

### Worst case

Assume:

- Long possession delay
- Repairs above estimate
- Lower rent
- Lower resale value
- Additional legal or carrying costs

Do not proceed if the worst case would threaten household finances.

## Compare With Alternatives

Compare the property with:

- CDT
- Broad ETF
- Bond ETF
- Savings account
- Other real-estate opportunities

The comparison should consider:

- Expected return
- Liquidity
- Time commitment
- Legal complexity
- Tax complexity
- Risk of permanent loss
- Income stability
- Currency exposure

## Required Inputs

Record:

- Winning bid
- Market value after possession
- Repair estimate
- Repair contingency
- Legal cost
- Possession cost
- Taxes and registration
- Administration debt
- Property-tax debt
- Utility debt
- Financing amount
- Interest rate
- Monthly carrying cost
- Expected months to possession
- Expected months to renovation
- Expected rent
- Expected vacancy
- Expected resale value
- Selling costs

## Decision Rules

Do not proceed when:

- Funding is uncertain
- Emergency reserves would be depleted
- Possession risk is not understood
- Title review is incomplete
- Repairs are uninspected and underfunded
- Profit depends on an optimistic resale value
- Rental income barely covers carrying costs
- The worst case is unaffordable
- Required return is lower than a simpler alternative after risk adjustment

## Personal Decision Questions

1. What is the maximum total cash I can commit?
2. How long can the money remain illiquid?
3. Can I absorb one year without rental income?
4. What repair overrun can I tolerate?
5. Would legal delays create stress?
6. Is the expected return high enough to justify the complexity?
7. Would a diversified ETF or CDT be easier and safer?
8. Is the property intended for residence, rent, or resale?
9. What is the exit if the original plan fails?
10. What conditions would make me walk away?
