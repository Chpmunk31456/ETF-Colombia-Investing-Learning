# Lesson 8 — Cross-Border Tax and Reporting Checklist

## Learning Objective

Understand the principal tax and information-reporting questions that may arise when a United States citizen living in Colombia owns Colombian accounts, investment funds, CDTs, stocks, bonds, ETFs, pensions, or foreign brokerage accounts.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

It does not determine:

- Tax residency
- Tax liability
- Filing obligations
- Treaty eligibility
- Foreign tax credits
- PFIC classification
- Estate-tax exposure
- The legal location of an asset

A qualified professional familiar with both United States and Colombian taxation should review the specific account and investment before purchase.

## Why Cross-Border Review Matters

A person may have obligations in more than one country.

Living in Colombia can create Colombian tax and information-reporting responsibilities.

United States citizens generally remain subject to US tax and information-reporting rules even while residing abroad.

An investment that appears simple from an investment perspective may create complex reporting obligations.

## Separate the Questions

For every account or investment, evaluate four separate questions:

1. Where is the investor tax resident?
2. Where is the account legally maintained?
3. Where is the asset considered located?
4. Which country taxes or requires reporting of the income or asset?

These questions may have different answers.

## Colombian Tax Residency

Do not assume that visa status alone determines tax residency.

Questions for a Colombian tax professional include:

- How many days was I physically present in Colombia?
- Does my family or economic center affect residency?
- When did Colombian tax residency begin?
- Am I required to report worldwide income?
- Are any transition rules applicable?
- What records prove the relevant travel dates?

## Colombian Worldwide Assets and Income

A Colombian tax resident may need professional guidance regarding:

- Foreign bank accounts
- Foreign brokerage accounts
- US-listed ETFs
- Foreign dividends
- Interest received abroad
- Capital gains
- Foreign pensions
- Foreign tax credits
- Currency conversion
- Asset valuation in Colombian pesos

## Colombian Foreign-Assets Declaration

DIAN states that qualifying Colombian taxpayers who hold foreign assets above the annual threshold may need to file Form 160.

For 2026, the published threshold is:

- More than 2,000 UVT
- COP 104,748,000 as of January 1, 2026

This is an information return.

It does not automatically mean that an additional tax is owed.

Professional review should determine:

- Whether the investor is a Colombian taxpayer required to file
- Which assets count as foreign assets
- How jointly owned assets are reported
- Which exchange rate is used
- How brokerage accounts are valued
- How securities acquired through Colombian market infrastructure are classified
- The applicable filing deadline

## United States Worldwide Income

A US citizen generally needs to evaluate US tax treatment of worldwide income, including:

- Colombian bank interest
- CDT interest
- Colombian dividends
- Foreign investment-fund distributions
- Capital gains
- Pension-related income
- Foreign brokerage income
- Currency transactions

Foreign income should not be omitted merely because tax was paid or withheld in Colombia.

## Foreign Tax Credits

Foreign tax credits may reduce double taxation in some circumstances.

Questions include:

- Was the foreign tax legally owed?
- Was it an income tax or another type of charge?
- Is the income classified the same way in both countries?
- Is the credit limited by income category?
- Was tax withheld at source?
- Are official tax certificates available?
- Is unused credit carried forward or back?

A foreign tax payment does not automatically create a dollar-for-dollar US credit.

## FBAR

The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts is commonly called the FBAR.

A US person may have an FBAR obligation when the aggregate value of reportable foreign financial accounts exceeds the applicable threshold at any point during the calendar year.

Potentially relevant accounts may include:

- Colombian savings accounts
- Checking accounts
- Certain brokerage accounts
- Certain investment accounts
- Accounts with signature authority
- Some jointly owned accounts

The aggregate maximum value is important.

The question is not limited to the year-end balance.

FBAR is filed separately from the federal income-tax return.

## Form 8938

Form 8938 reports specified foreign financial assets when the applicable threshold is exceeded.

For qualifying taxpayers living outside the United States, thresholds differ according to marital and filing status.

Examples of potentially reportable assets include:

- Foreign bank accounts
- Foreign brokerage accounts
- Foreign-issued stocks or bonds
- Interests in foreign entities
- Certain foreign pensions
- Certain foreign insurance or annuity contracts

Form 8938 is generally attached to the federal tax return.

Filing Form 8938 does not replace an FBAR filing obligation.

## Form 8621 and PFIC Risk

PFIC means Passive Foreign Investment Company.

A US person who owns an interest in a PFIC may need to evaluate Form 8621 reporting.

Foreign pooled investments that may require PFIC review include:

- Non-US mutual funds
- Non-US ETFs
- Some Colombian collective investment funds
- Certain foreign money-market funds
- Some foreign investment companies

PFIC consequences can include:

- Complex annual reporting
- Special tax calculations
- Unfavorable treatment of some distributions and gains
- Separate analysis for each fund
- Significant professional preparation costs

Do not purchase a Colombian or other non-US pooled fund merely because it appears similar to a US ETF.

Obtain written PFIC guidance first.

## US-Domiciled Versus Non-US-Domiciled ETFs

The exchange where an ETF trades does not by itself determine fund domicile.

Before purchasing an ETF, record:

- Legal fund name
- Fund domicile
- Issuer
- Exchange
- Ticker
- International Securities Identification Number
- Prospectus
- Tax classification
- Distribution policy

A US-listed, US-domiciled ETF may present different US tax considerations from a non-US-domiciled ETF.

However, other issues may still apply, including:

- Colombian taxation
- Foreign brokerage reporting
- Dividend withholding
- Estate planning
- Currency exposure

## Colombian Collective Investment Funds

Before purchasing a Colombian collective investment fund, ask:

- Is the fund a foreign corporation for US tax purposes?
- Could it meet a PFIC income or asset test?
- Will the manager provide annual tax information suitable for US reporting?
- Is a Qualified Electing Fund election possible?
- Is mark-to-market treatment available?
- What will annual Form 8621 preparation cost?
- Does the expected investment return justify the compliance burden?

## CDTs

A CDT may create questions concerning:

- Colombian interest taxation
- Withholding at source
- US worldwide-income reporting
- Foreign tax credits
- FBAR reporting if held through a reportable account
- Form 8938 reporting
- COP-to-USD income conversion
- Accrued versus paid interest
- Renewal transactions

Keep:

- Opening certificate
- Principal amount
- Interest rate
- Maturity date
- Withholding certificate
- Renewal statement
- Closing balance
- Exchange-rate documentation

## Colombian Pension or Retirement Products

A Colombian voluntary pension or retirement product may require analysis under several US regimes.

Questions can include:

- Is it treated as a foreign trust?
- Is it a foreign financial account?
- Is the underlying investment a PFIC?
- Does a reporting exception apply?
- Are contributions currently deductible?
- Is growth currently taxable?
- How are withdrawals characterized?
- Are Forms 3520 or 3520-A relevant?
- Is Form 8938 relevant?
- Is FBAR relevant?

Do not assume that Colombian tax-favored treatment is recognized by the United States.

## Brokerage Location Versus Asset Location

A brokerage account and its underlying holdings may require separate analysis.

Examples:

- A Colombian brokerage account holding US securities
- A US brokerage account holding foreign securities
- A Colombian platform providing access to foreign securities
- A foreign broker holding US-domiciled ETFs

For every structure, identify:

- Account jurisdiction
- Broker legal entity
- Custodian
- Security issuer
- Fund domicile
- Trading exchange
- Settlement system
- Reporting classification

## Currency Conversion and Recordkeeping

US returns generally require amounts to be reported in US dollars.

Colombian reporting may require values in Colombian pesos.

Maintain records of:

- Transaction date
- Original currency
- Original amount
- Exchange rate
- Exchange-rate source
- USD value
- COP value
- Fees
- Taxes withheld
- Settlement date

Use a consistent, supportable exchange-rate method approved by the relevant tax professional.

## Cost Basis

Maintain cost-basis records independently of the broker.

For each purchase, record:

- Security or product
- Purchase date
- Quantity
- Unit price
- Commission
- Currency-conversion cost
- Total cost in original currency
- USD tax basis
- COP tax basis
- Exchange-rate source
- Reinvestment transactions

Brokers in different countries may not calculate tax basis according to the same rules.

## Dividends and Distributions

Record:

- Gross dividend
- Foreign tax withheld
- Net amount received
- Payment date
- Currency
- USD conversion
- COP conversion
- Whether the payment was a dividend, interest, capital distribution, or return of capital

Do not rely only on the net cash deposited.

## Capital Gains

A sale may require separate calculations for:

- US tax
- Colombian tax
- Currency conversion
- Foreign tax credits
- Holding period
- Cost basis
- Fees
- Wash-sale or loss-limitation rules
- PFIC treatment

A transaction can show a gain in one currency and a different result in another currency.

## Estate and Beneficiary Questions

Cross-border investments can also affect:

- Beneficiary designations
- Probate
- Colombian succession procedures
- US estate-tax exposure
- Joint ownership
- Account access after death
- Transfer restrictions
- Required legal documents

Before opening an account, confirm that beneficiary information is permitted and correctly recorded.

## Pre-Purchase Stop Rule

Do not purchase a non-US ETF, mutual fund, collective investment fund, pension product, or pooled investment until:

- The legal domicile is confirmed
- PFIC risk is reviewed
- Annual reporting requirements are understood
- Expected tax-preparation costs are estimated
- Colombian treatment is reviewed
- The compliance burden is compared with the expected investment benefit

## Beginner Rules

1. Treat tax reporting as part of investment cost.
2. Never assume local tax treatment controls US treatment.
3. Never assume US treatment controls Colombian treatment.
4. Verify legal fund domicile.
5. Keep maximum annual account balances.
6. Retain gross income and withholding records.
7. Do not rely entirely on broker-generated tax reports.
8. Obtain written PFIC analysis before buying non-US pooled funds.
9. Maintain records in both USD and COP when appropriate.
10. Review reporting obligations annually because thresholds and rules can change.
