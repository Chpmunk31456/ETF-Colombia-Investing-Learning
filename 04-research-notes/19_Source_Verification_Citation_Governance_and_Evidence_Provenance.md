# Lesson 47 — Source Verification, Citation Governance, and Evidence Provenance

## Learning Objective

Learn how to identify authoritative sources, preserve evidence provenance, distinguish facts from estimates and opinions, document verification dates, manage conflicting information, and prevent unsupported or outdated claims from entering investment, tax, property, insurance, or financial-planning decisions.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

Legal, tax, regulatory, financial-product, insurance, property, and institutional information may change.

Current decisions should be verified through authoritative sources and qualified professionals where appropriate.

## Core Principle

A statement is not reliable merely because it:

- Appears online
- is repeated often
- comes from a polished website
- appears in an AI response
- comes from a salesperson
- appears in a social-media post
- was correct several years ago
- is included in an unofficial summary
- supports the desired conclusion

Every material claim should answer:

- What is the source?
- Who created it?
- When was it issued?
- When was it reviewed?
- What jurisdiction applies?
- What exactly does it support?
- Is it still current?
- Is it independent?
- Can another person retrieve it?
- Does conflicting evidence exist?

## Evidence Provenance

Evidence provenance describes where information came from and how it moved from its original source into:

- A research note
- a calculation
- a comparison
- a decision record
- a template
- a publication
- a professional consultation

Good provenance allows another reviewer to reconstruct the reasoning.

## Primary Source

A primary source is the original or authoritative record.

Examples:

- Statute
- regulation
- official tax guidance
- signed contract
- policy wording
- account statement
- trade confirmation
- official fund prospectus
- property title certificate
- court notice
- official auction record
- regulator database
- original research paper

## Secondary Source

A secondary source explains or summarizes primary material.

Examples:

- Educational article
- law-firm summary
- financial news article
- textbook
- analyst report
- comparison website

Secondary sources may be useful for understanding but should not replace primary verification for material decisions.

## Tertiary Source

A tertiary source aggregates or simplifies information.

Examples:

- General encyclopedia
- search-result summary
- discussion forum
- unsourced checklist
- social-media post
- AI-generated overview

Tertiary sources may help identify research questions but should rarely be the final authority for material claims.

## Source Hierarchy

A conservative hierarchy may be:

1. Applicable law or official legal record
2. regulator or government authority
3. signed contract or official account record
4. official issuer or institution document
5. qualified professional opinion
6. peer-reviewed or formal research
7. reputable secondary analysis
8. commercial comparison
9. media report
10. forum, social post, or unsourced claim

The correct hierarchy depends on the question.

## Authority

Source authority asks whether the creator has recognized responsibility or competence for the information.

Examples:

- Tax authority for tax-filing requirements
- regulator for licensing status
- broker for account-specific settlement records
- insurer for policy wording
- land registry for title records
- issuer for fund prospectus
- court for judicial process records

## Independence

A source may be authoritative about its own product but not independent.

Examples:

- Fund issuer describes its ETF
- property seller describes the property
- contractor describes repair needs
- adviser describes recommended products
- insurer describes policy benefits

Independent verification may still be required.

## Currency of Information

Record:

- Publication date
- effective date
- last updated date
- access date
- next review date

A webpage access date does not prove the content is current.

## Effective Date

The effective date identifies when a rule, contract term, rate, or condition applies.

A document may be published before or after its effective date.

## Version

Record the document version where available.

Examples:

- Policy edition
- contract revision
- prospectus version
- regulatory circular number
- technical standard edition
- spreadsheet revision
- appraisal version

## Jurisdiction

Every legal, tax, property, insurance, and regulatory source should identify jurisdiction.

Possible classifications include:

- Colombia
- United States
- state or department
- municipality
- cross-border
- international
- contract-specific
- institution-specific

## Claim

A claim is a statement presented as factual or analytical.

Examples:

- A fee is 1%.
- A CDT matures on a stated date.
- A property has a lien.
- A fund tracks a particular index.
- A tax filing is required.
- A policy excludes a specified event.
- A broker account is covered by a particular protection scheme.

## Claim Classification

Classify each material claim as:

- Verified fact
- provisionally verified
- estimate
- assumption
- opinion
- interpretation
- professional advice
- unresolved
- contradicted
- outdated

## Verified Fact

A verified fact is supported by sufficient current evidence.

The verification record should identify:

- Source
- date
- exact supporting passage or field
- reviewer
- scope
- limitations

## Estimate

An estimate is a calculated or judgment-based value that is not directly known.

Examples:

- Future property maintenance
- expected vacancy
- renovation contingency
- future inflation
- resale value
- professional fees
- investment return

Estimates should include:

- Method
- source inputs
- range
- confidence
- review date

## Assumption

An assumption is a condition treated as true for analysis.

Examples:

- Property remains occupied
- inflation averages a selected rate
- vehicle is held for ten years
- income continues
- exchange rate remains within a range

Assumptions should not be presented as verified facts.

## Opinion

An opinion expresses judgment.

Examples:

- A provider appears responsive.
- A property layout is attractive.
- A portfolio feels too complex.
- A renovation appears high quality.

Opinions may be relevant but should be labeled.

## Interpretation

An interpretation explains what a source may mean.

Legal, tax, contract, and policy interpretations should identify:

- Source text
- interpreter
- qualifications
- uncertainty
- conflicting interpretations
- need for professional review

## Citation

A citation connects a claim to its supporting source.

A useful citation record should include:

- Source title
- source organization
- document type
- date
- URL or secure record location
- section, page, paragraph, or field
- access date
- supported claim
- reviewer
- source status

## Citation Precision

A citation should point as closely as possible to the supporting information.

Prefer:

- Page
- section
- article
- paragraph
- table
- account-statement line
- contract clause
- registry entry

Avoid citing an entire large document without identifying the relevant location.

## Citation Completeness

A citation should allow another reviewer to retrieve or locate the evidence.

Incomplete citation:

- “Bank website”

Stronger citation:

- Institution name
- document title
- fee schedule
- effective date
- section
- access date
- archived copy or official URL

## Citation Does Not Prove the Claim

A citation may be:

- Irrelevant
- outdated
- misquoted
- taken out of context
- based on an unreliable source
- inconsistent with the claim
- superseded

The reviewer must confirm that the source actually supports the statement.

## Quotation

Use short quotations only when exact wording matters.

Examples:

- Contract clause
- policy exclusion
- regulatory definition
- official decision

Preserve context and avoid excessive copying.

## Paraphrase

A paraphrase restates information in original language.

A paraphrase still requires citation.

It should not change the source meaning.

## Source Record

For each source, record:

- Source ID
- title
- organization
- author
- category
- jurisdiction
- publication date
- effective date
- access date
- version
- URL or private location
- authority rating
- independence rating
- current status
- notes

## Source Authority Rating

Possible ratings:

### High

Official, primary, applicable, and current.

### Moderate

Credible and relevant but secondary, incomplete, or not independently authoritative.

### Low

Commercial, promotional, informal, outdated, or difficult to verify.

### Unacceptable

Anonymous, falsified, materially misleading, inaccessible, or unrelated.

## Evidence Confidence

Possible confidence levels:

- High
- Moderate
- Low
- Unknown

Confidence should consider:

- Source quality
- currentness
- completeness
- consistency
- independence
- directness
- verification

## Claim-to-Source Mapping

Each material claim should be connected to at least one source.

High-risk claims may require:

- Primary source
- independent confirmation
- professional review

## Single-Source Risk

Reliance on one source can be dangerous when:

- Financial exposure is material
- source has a conflict
- information is promotional
- law or tax is involved
- title or ownership is involved
- payment instructions changed
- fraud is possible
- document authenticity is uncertain

## Corroboration

Corroboration means confirming information through another reliable source.

Examples:

- Broker statement plus bank statement
- title record plus attorney review
- invoice plus completed work
- fund prospectus plus regulator record
- professional license plus regulator database
- contract clause plus written professional interpretation

## Conflicting Sources

When sources disagree:

1. Record each source.
2. identify the exact conflict.
3. compare dates.
4. compare jurisdiction.
5. compare authority.
6. compare document version.
7. determine whether one supersedes another.
8. seek professional review if material.
9. label the issue unresolved until supported.
10. avoid selecting the preferred answer merely because it is favorable.

## Superseded Source

A superseded source has been replaced by a newer authoritative version.

Record:

- Superseding source
- effective date
- affected claims
- files requiring update
- review status

## Archived Source

An archived source is preserved for historical evidence but should not be used as current authority unless appropriate.

## Broken Source

A source may become unavailable because:

- URL changed
- page removed
- portal access ended
- document expired
- institution merged
- file moved
- account closed

Preserve important documents securely when legally permitted.

## Web Source Preservation

For important public web evidence, retain where appropriate:

- Page title
- organization
- URL
- access date
- relevant excerpt
- PDF or official download
- screenshot
- archive reference
- version or effective date

Do not rely only on browser bookmarks.

## Private Evidence

Private evidence may include:

- Bank statement
- account confirmation
- contract
- tax filing
- medical invoice
- property record
- professional memorandum
- identification document

Private evidence should be:

- Stored securely
- excluded from public Git
- referenced with a non-sensitive identifier
- access controlled
- retained according to applicable requirements

## Evidence Identifier

Use a non-sensitive identifier such as:

- SRC-001
- PROP-LEGAL-003
- TAX-US-2026-002
- BROKER-STATEMENT-004

Do not place account numbers or personal identifiers in evidence IDs.

## Source File Naming

A secure private naming convention may be:

`YYYY-MM-DD_Source_DocumentType_Reference`

Example:

`2026-08-01_ExampleBroker_FeeSchedule_SRC-014.pdf`

## Document Authenticity

Authenticity checks may include:

- Official domain
- digital signature
- document number
- registry confirmation
- issuer contact
- file metadata
- QR or verification code
- direct portal download
- professional confirmation

A realistic-looking PDF may still be false.

## Screenshot Limitation

A screenshot may show what appeared on a screen but may not prove:

- Source authenticity
- complete context
- current status
- unchanged content
- legal validity

Use screenshots as supporting evidence rather than sole proof where possible.

## AI-Generated Content

AI-generated text should be treated as:

- Draft assistance
- research support
- question generation
- summarization requiring verification

It should not be treated automatically as:

- Primary authority
- legal opinion
- tax opinion
- current regulatory source
- account-specific fact
- property-title evidence
- professional certification

## AI Verification Rule

Before relying on AI-assisted content:

1. Identify material factual claims.
2. identify time-sensitive claims.
3. identify legal or tax claims.
4. locate authoritative sources.
5. compare the claim with the source.
6. correct unsupported statements.
7. record sources.
8. label remaining uncertainty.
9. obtain professional review where required.

## Evidence Review Date

Every time-sensitive source should have a next review date.

Possible review triggers include:

- Annual review
- transaction
- product renewal
- policy renewal
- tax year
- legal change
- interest-rate change
- property purchase
- sale

## Stale Evidence

Evidence may be stale when:

- Product terms changed
- law changed
- tax year changed
- institution changed
- policy renewed
- valuation aged
- professional license expired
- property condition changed
- account status changed

## Staleness Classification

Use:

- Current
- review soon
- stale
- superseded
- archived
- unable to verify

## Materiality

A source issue is material when it could affect:

- Investment decision
- tax obligation
- legal right
- ownership
- insurance recovery
- financial loss
- transaction validity
- retirement security
- household welfare

## Citation Review

Before publication or decision use, confirm:

- Claim exists
- citation exists
- source is relevant
- source is current
- source supports claim
- jurisdiction matches
- quotation is accurate
- assumptions are labeled
- private evidence is not exposed
- conflicts are disclosed

## Evidence Quality Status

### Green

- Primary or strong source
- current
- relevant
- traceable
- claim supported
- no material conflict

### Yellow

- Secondary source
- partial support
- approaching review date
- unresolved ambiguity
- independent confirmation recommended

### Red

- Missing source
- unsupported claim
- outdated authority
- jurisdiction mismatch
- conflicting primary sources
- document authenticity concern
- private evidence exposed

## Evidence Exception

Create an exception when:

- Source is temporarily unavailable
- official record is pending
- estimate must be used
- independent confirmation cannot yet be obtained
- professional review is delayed
- document authenticity remains under review

The exception should include:

- Risk
- temporary assumption
- compensating control
- expiration
- required final evidence

## Source Retraction or Correction

When a source is corrected or withdrawn:

1. Identify affected claims.
2. stop relying on the source.
3. update research notes.
4. update calculations.
5. update published material.
6. document correction.
7. notify decision owners where material.
8. retain prior evidence for audit history.

## Publication Citation Governance

A publication candidate should identify:

- Sources supporting time-sensitive statements
- source access dates
- jurisdiction
- known limitations
- unresolved interpretations
- professional-review needs

## Citation Style

The repository may use a simplified source-note style.

Example:

`[SRC-014] Example Institution, Fee Schedule, effective 2026-07-01, section 3, accessed 2026-08-01.`

Consistency is more important than decorative formatting.

## Source Register Relationship

The citation should point to a source record.

The source record should identify where the evidence is stored or retrieved.

The claim register should identify what the source supports.

## Repository Privacy Rule

Public files may contain:

- Public source title
- public URL
- organization
- date
- general citation

Public files should not contain:

- Private portal URL
- account identifier
- personal document
- private correspondence
- nonpublic professional memorandum
- sensitive screenshot

## Automatic Stop Conditions

Do not rely on or publish a material claim when:

- No source exists
- source cannot be retrieved
- source does not support the claim
- source is outdated
- jurisdiction does not match
- official sources conflict
- document authenticity is uncertain
- estimate is presented as fact
- AI output is the only authority
- private evidence would be exposed
- professional review is required but incomplete

## Beginner Rules

1. Start with the original source.
2. Record the date and jurisdiction.
3. Distinguish facts, assumptions, estimates, and opinions.
4. Cite the exact section that supports the claim.
5. Use independent confirmation for material decisions.
6. Preserve important source documents.
7. treat AI content as a draft requiring verification.
8. review sources when rules or products change.
9. document conflicting evidence.
10. stop when a material claim cannot be supported.
