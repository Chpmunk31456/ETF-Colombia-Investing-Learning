# Lesson 49 — Financial Decision Journaling, Pre-Mortems, and Outcome Review

## Learning Objective

Learn how to document financial decisions before acting, identify assumptions and behavioral risks, conduct a pre-mortem, define success and stop conditions, and later evaluate decision quality separately from investment outcome.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

A decision journal does not predict markets, guarantee results, replace professional advice, or eliminate financial risk.

Material legal, tax, investment, insurance, debt, and property decisions may require review by qualified professionals.

## Core Principle

A good result does not prove that the decision process was good.

A poor result does not prove that the decision process was poor.

Financial decisions should be evaluated using:

- Information available at the time
- quality of evidence
- assumptions
- alternatives considered
- risks identified
- policy compliance
- professional review
- execution quality
- outcome
- lessons learned

## Decision Quality Versus Outcome Quality

Use four categories:

| Decision Quality | Outcome | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Good | Good | Strong process and favorable result |
| Good | Poor | Sound process but unfavorable uncertainty |
| Poor | Good | Weak process with fortunate result |
| Poor | Poor | Weak process and unfavorable result |

A lucky result should not validate a weak process.

## Decision Journal

A decision journal records what was believed before the outcome became known.

It helps prevent:

- Hindsight bias
- selective memory
- outcome bias
- story rewriting
- repeated mistakes
- unsupported confidence

## Decision Statement

The decision statement should be specific.

Weak:

- Decide whether this investment is good.

Stronger:

- Decide whether to invest COP 20 million in the proposed ETF through the verified brokerage account while maintaining the approved emergency reserve and portfolio-allocation limits.

Examples:

- Preserve purchasing power
- generate retirement income
- reduce debt cost

- acquire a rental property
- improve portfolio diversification
- simplify accounts
- reduce currency mismatch
- protect against financial loss

## Alternatives

At least three alternatives should normally be considered:

1. Proceed as proposed.
2. Modify the proposal.
3. Delay.
4. Take no action.
5. Select another product or provider.
6. Reduce the amount.
7. Use cash instead of debt.
8. Use debt instead of liquidating assets.
9. Obtain additional evidence.
10. Seek another professional opinion.

Doing nothing is a valid alternative and should be evaluated.

- risk

- tax effect
- liquidity effect
- behavioral effect

Do not treat the status quo as risk free.

## Mandatory Criteria

Mandatory criteria cannot be offset by a high total score.

Examples:

- Emergency reserve remains intact

- fraud stop conditions absent

## Weighted Criteria

## Evidence

For each important conclusion, record:

- Claim
- source
- date
- confidence
- limitation

## Assumption Register

Every decision should identify assumptions.

Examples:

- Income continues
- pension remains stable
- property remains rentable
- maintenance remains within estimate
- currency remains within tested range
- interest rate remains fixed
- investment remains liquid
- provider completes work on time
- insurance remains available

## Assumption Confidence

Use:

- High
- Moderate
- Low
- Unknown

Low-confidence assumptions should receive:

- Wider scenario ranges
- stronger contingency
- lower exposure
- additional verification
- delay or rejection

## Prediction

A prediction is a forecast about what may happen.

Examples:

- Property value may rise
- ETF may outperform cash
- currency may strengthen
- interest rates may fall
- rental demand may improve

Predictions should not be disguised as facts.

## Probability

When probabilities are used, record:

- Estimated probability
- source or reasoning
- confidence
- range
- conditions that could change it

Avoid false precision.

Example:

Prefer:

- Moderate likelihood, approximately 40% to 60%

Rather than:

- Exactly 53.7% likelihood

unless strong quantitative evidence supports the precision.

## Base Rate

A base rate is the general frequency of an outcome in a relevant comparison group.

Base rates may improve decisions involving:

- Renovation overruns
- business survival
- tenant vacancy
- investment underperformance
- fraud
- project delay
- loan delinquency

The comparison group must be relevant.

## Inside View

The inside view focuses on the specific details of the current case.

## Outside View

The outside view compares the case with similar historical situations.

Strong decisions consider both.

## Pre-Mortem

A pre-mortem assumes the decision was made and later failed.

The reviewer asks:

- What most likely caused the failure?

This can reveal risks that ordinary optimism overlooks.

## Pre-Mortem Process

1. Define the proposed decision.
2. imagine that it failed materially.
3. list possible causes.
4. rank causes by likelihood and impact.
5. identify warning indicators.
6. identify preventive controls.
7. define contingency actions.
8. decide whether exposure should be reduced.
9. update stop conditions.
10. reconsider the decision.

## Failure Statement

Write a specific failure statement.

Example:

- Twelve months after purchasing the property, the investment has created a material cash-flow deficit and cannot be sold without a substantial loss.

Then ask why.

## Failure Cause

Possible causes include:

## Early Warning Indicator

An early warning indicator signals increasing risk before full failure.

Examples:

- Renovation spending exceeds 50% of contingency
- vacancy exceeds expected period
- rent collection declines
- reserve falls below policy
- provider misses two milestones
- account reconciliation difference remains unresolved
- investment exceeds concentration limit
- debt-service ratio rises
- insurance renewal is denied
- currency exposure exceeds approved range

## Trigger Threshold

A trigger threshold defines when action is required.

Example:

- If the repair budget exceeds the approved amount by 10%, stop new work and require a revised scope and funding review.

## Contingency Plan

A contingency plan identifies what to do if a risk occurs.

Possible actions include:

- Reduce exposure
- pause project
- increase reserve
- refinance
- sell asset
- replace provider
- invoke contract rights
- file insurance claim
- reduce discretionary spending
- delay another goal
- obtain legal review

## Reversibility

Classify a decision as:

- Easily reversible
- reversible with moderate cost
- difficult to reverse
- effectively irreversible

Less reversible decisions require stronger evidence and review.

## Optionality

Examples:

- Keeping liquidity
- using a small pilot

- negotiating an exit clause
- using staged payments
- selecting transferable investments
- avoiding unnecessary long-term debt

- short-term rental-management trial

## Pilot Success Criteria

- success condition

- expansion decision

## Expected Value

A simplified expected-value calculation equals:

Probability of each outcome
multiplied by its financial result
summed across outcomes

Expected value does not capture every important risk.

## Example

| Outcome | Probability | Result | Weighted Result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Strong result | 25% | COP 40 million | COP 10 million |
| Moderate result | 50% | COP 10 million | COP 5 million |
| Loss | 25% | -COP 30 million | -COP 7.5 million |

Estimated expected value:

COP 7.5 million

This does not show:

- Liquidity
- timing
- tax
- emotional effect
- catastrophic loss
- estimation error

## Expected Utility

Expected utility considers that losses and gains may affect the household differently.

Losing COP 30 million may be more harmful than gaining COP 30 million is beneficial.

## Maximum Acceptable Loss

Define the maximum acceptable:

- Financial loss
- monthly cash-flow deficit
- reserve reduction
- time delay
- debt increase
- legal exposure

## Ruin Risk

Ruin risk is the possibility that a loss creates irreversible financial harm.

Examples:

- Loss of essential housing
- inability to fund health care
- depletion of retirement reserves
- unmanageable debt
- loss of legal ownership
- tax enforcement
- loss of emergency liquidity

Avoid decisions with unacceptable ruin risk even when expected return appears attractive.

## Margin of Safety

A margin of safety provides room for error.

Possible margins include:

- Lower purchase price
- larger reserve
- conservative income estimate
- higher expense estimate
- lower debt
- longer timeline
- independent inspection
- stronger insurance
- staged commitment

## Sensitivity Analysis

Test what happens when key assumptions change.

Examples:

- Return is lower
- vacancy is longer
- renovation is more expensive
- interest rates rise
- income falls

- tax is higher

## Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias favors information supporting the preferred conclusion.

Control:

- Require contrary evidence.
- assign a reviewer to challenge the proposal.
- document reasons not to proceed.

## Anchoring

Anchoring gives excessive importance to an initial figure.

Examples:

- Asking price
- original purchase price
- advertised return
- first repair estimate
- prior exchange rate

Control:

- Use independent valuation.
- calculate from current evidence.
- review multiple estimates.

## Overconfidence

Overconfidence overstates knowledge, skill, or forecasting accuracy.

Control:

- Use ranges.
- compare with base rates.
- record confidence.
- obtain independent review.
- reduce exposure.

## Recency Bias

Recency bias gives excessive weight to recent events.

Examples:

- Recent market gain
- recent currency move
- recent property sale
- recent interest-rate change

Control:

- Review longer history.
- test multiple environments.
- avoid extrapolating one period.

## Loss Aversion

Loss aversion can cause:

- Refusal to sell a weak investment
- excessive avoidance of reasonable risk
- holding an unsuitable property
- doubling down to avoid admitting loss

## Sunk-Cost Fallacy

Past spending should not determine whether additional money is committed.

Ask:

- Would the remaining investment be approved today if prior spending had not occurred?

## Fear of Missing Out

Fear of missing out can create:

- Rushed purchase
- skipped due diligence
- excessive bid
- concentration
- use of emergency reserves

Control:

- Cooling-off period
- written maximum price
- no same-day commitment
- independent review

## Authority Bias

Authority bias occurs when a claim is accepted because of the speaker’s title, confidence, or status.

Control:

- Verify credentials.
- inspect evidence.
- identify conflicts.
- obtain second opinion.

## Action Bias

Action bias favors doing something even when waiting is better.

Control:

- Include no action as an explicit alternative.

## Red-Team Review

A red-team reviewer challenges the proposed decision.

Questions include:

- What evidence is weakest?
- What is being assumed?
- Who benefits?
- What could be fraudulent?
- What cost is missing?
- What would make this unaffordable?
- What would cause permanent loss?
- Which professional opinion is absent?
- What would change the recommendation?

## Devil’s Advocate

A devil’s advocate intentionally argues against the preferred decision.

This role should be assigned before final approval.

## Decision Deadline

## Hindsight Bias

Hindsight bias makes an outcome appear more predictable after it occurs.

Control:

- Review the original dated journal.
- use information available at the decision date.
- avoid judging based on later facts.

## Outcome Bias

Outcome bias judges decision quality primarily by the result.

Example:

- A speculative investment increased, so the decision is considered sound.

The process may still have violated policy and exposed the household to unacceptable loss.

## Calibration

Calibration compares predicted confidence with actual outcomes over many decisions.

Example:

If decisions labeled 80% likely succeed only half the time, confidence may be overstated.

Small samples should be interpreted cautiously.

## Automatic Stop Conditions

Do not approve a material decision when:

- Objective is unclear
- alternatives were not considered
- mandatory condition failed
- evidence is missing
- material claim is unsupported
- low-confidence assumption drives the result
- pre-mortem identifies unacceptable ruin risk
- emergency reserve would be compromised
- transaction is driven by urgency or fear of missing out
- required professional review is incomplete
- household decision-maker is impaired or coerced
- payment instructions are unverified
- execution would exceed approved limits

## Beginner Rules

1. Write the decision before knowing the outcome.
2. Separate decision quality from outcome quality.
3. Include doing nothing as an alternative.
4. label assumptions and predictions.
5. conduct a pre-mortem.
6. define early warnings and stop conditions.
7. use smaller exposure when confidence is low.
8. require stronger review for irreversible decisions.
9. compare execution with approval.
10. review outcomes without rewriting history.
