# Lesson 39 — Investment Governance, Policy Exceptions, and Annual Control Testing

## Learning Objective

Learn how to govern a personal or household investment program through documented responsibilities, policy compliance, exception management, control testing, evidence retention, issue remediation, and annual approval.

## Core Principle

A policy has little value when compliance is never tested.

Investment governance requires evidence that:

- Decisions followed approved rules
- Required reviews occurred
- Exceptions were documented
- Accounts were reconciled
- Security controls remained active
- Professional advice was obtained where required
- Problems were corrected
- Policies were updated when circumstances changed

## Governance

Governance defines:

- Who makes decisions
- Who reviews material decisions
- Which rules apply
- What evidence is required
- Which actions require professional review
- How exceptions are approved
- How problems are corrected
- How results are reported

## Personal Investment Governance

A household investment-governance structure may include:

- Decision owner
- Spouse or household reviewer
- Trusted contact
- Tax professional
- Attorney
- Investment professional
- Property professional
- Executor or incapacity agent

The structure should remain understandable and proportionate.

## Governance Objectives

The governance system should help:

- Protect essential household finances
- Preserve emergency liquidity
- Prevent fraud
- Control investment concentration
- Maintain accurate records
- Identify legal and tax questions
- Reduce emotional decisions
- Support continuity during incapacity
- Document why actions were taken
- Detect control failures early

## Governance Documents

Possible governing documents include:

- Investment Policy Statement
- Liquidity policy

- Portfolio-allocation policy
- Rebalancing policy
- Property-investment criteria
- Fraud stop rules
- Data-classification policy
- Backup and recovery plan
- Estate and incapacity plan
- Professional-review requirements

## Policy Hierarchy

A simple hierarchy may be:

### Level 1 — Governing principles

Examples:

- Protect essential expenses
- Do not use emergency reserves for investing
- Do not transact through unverified institutions

### Level 2 — Formal policies

Examples:

- Investment Policy Statement
- Liquidity limits
- Allocation limits
- Maximum property exposure

### Level 3 — Procedures

Examples:

- Pre-trade checklist
- Monthly reconciliation
- Property due diligence
- Annual insurance review

### Level 4 — Records

Examples:

- Decision journal
- Trade register
- Professional advice register
- Exception log
- Annual review report

## Roles and Responsibilities

Define responsibility for:

- Final investment decision
- Account access
- Recordkeeping
- Monthly reconciliation
- Tax-document collection
- Professional coordination
- Property oversight
- Backup testing
- Beneficiary review
- Emergency access

## Decision Owner

The decision owner is responsible for:

- Defining the objective
- Reviewing evidence
- Confirming affordability
- Completing decision gates
- Approving or rejecting the action
- Recording the decision
- Scheduling review

## Independent Reviewer

An independent reviewer may:

- Challenge assumptions
- Check calculations
- Confirm limits
- identify missing evidence
- review conflicts
- confirm professional advice
- verify that stop conditions were considered

The reviewer should not merely agree with the decision owner.

## Trusted Contact

A trusted contact may assist when there is concern involving:

- Fraud
- incapacity
- unusual withdrawals
- account-access loss
- emergency hospitalization
- cognitive impairment
- coercion
- unexplained transaction activity

A trusted contact does not automatically have authority to transact.

## Segregation of Duties

Segregation of duties means separating incompatible responsibilities where practical.

Examples:

- One person proposes a material transfer and another verifies instructions.
- A contractor submits an invoice and the owner verifies completed work.
- A property manager collects rent and the owner reconciles statements.
- A broker executes trades and the investor independently reviews confirmations.

## Governance Calendar

Establish recurring reviews.

### Monthly

- Cash and liquidity review

- Unusual-transaction review
- Reserve review

### Quarterly

- Allocation and drift review

- Property cash flow

### Semiannual

- Beneficiary and trusted-contact review
- Insurance review where needed
- Account-security review
- Backup review
- Property inspection or condition review

### Annual

- Investment Policy Statement review
- Portfolio performance report
- Full control assessment
- Net-worth statement
- tax-document inventory
- Estate and incapacity review
- Insurance review
- Professional-advice review
- Policy approval

## Control

A control is an action designed to prevent, detect, or correct risk.

## Preventive Controls

Prevent a problem before it occurs.

Examples:

- Position limits
- MFA
- cooling-off period
- verified payment instructions
- professional contract review
- maximum auction bid
- segregation of duties

## Detective Controls

Identify a problem after or while it occurs.

Examples:

- Account reconciliation
- transaction alerts
- policy-compliance review
- secret scanning
- title search
- statement review
- performance reconciliation

## Corrective Controls

Resolve a detected problem.

Examples:

- Credential rotation
- transaction dispute
- portfolio rebalancing
- corrected tax filing
- debt restructuring
- contractor defect correction
- policy update

## Manual Controls

Manual controls require human action.

Examples:

- Reviewing a trade confirmation
- completing a checklist
- comparing bank statements
- approving a contractor invoice

Manual controls may fail because of:

- Fatigue
- omission
- misunderstanding
- time pressure
- lack of evidence

## Automated Controls

Automated controls may include:

- Transaction alerts
- recurring backups
- calendar reminders
- spreadsheet validation
- account notifications
- secret scanning
- automatic reserve transfers

Automated controls should still be tested.

## Key Controls

A key control is especially important because its failure could cause material harm.

Possible key controls include:

- Emergency reserve protection
- payment-instruction verification
- broker reconciliation
- professional tax review
- property-title review
- maximum bid approval
- MFA
- backup recovery test
- beneficiary review
- investment decision gate

## Control Inventory

For every key control, record:

- Control name
- Risk addressed
- Owner
- Frequency
- Evidence
- Last completion date
- Next due date
- Test procedure
- Current status
- Remediation required

## Control Design

A control is properly designed when it could reasonably address the intended risk.

Example:

Risk:

- Sending funds to a fraudulent account

Weak control:

- Confirming instructions in the same email that announced the change

Stronger control:

- Independently contacting the institution through a previously verified channel

## Control Operation

A well-designed control may still fail if it is not performed.

Questions include:

- Was it completed?
- Was it completed on time?
- Was the correct information reviewed?
- Was evidence retained?
- Was the reviewer qualified?
- Were exceptions escalated?

## Control Evidence

Possible evidence includes:

- Completed checklist
- dated statement
- trade confirmation
- screenshot
- professional opinion
- email confirmation
- title certificate
- reconciliation
- meeting notes
- backup test record
- signed approval

Evidence should be sufficient to show what occurred without exposing unnecessary private data.

## Evidence Quality

Possible ratings:

### Strong

- Official, complete, current, and independently verifiable

### Moderate

- Reliable but incomplete or internally produced

### Weak

- Informal, outdated, promotional, or unsupported

## Control Test

A control test evaluates whether the control:

- Exists
- Is properly designed
- Was performed
- Was timely
- Produced evidence
- Detected relevant problems
- Resulted in correction where necessary

## Test Population

The population is the full group of items subject to a control.

Examples:

- All trades during the year
- All property payments
- All professional consultations
- All investment decisions above a threshold
- All account statements
- All policy exceptions

## Sample Testing

When the population is large, select a sample.

Possible sample criteria include:

- Largest transactions
- Highest-risk transactions
- New institutions
- unusual transactions
- random items
- transactions near limits
- transactions with prior exceptions

For a small personal portfolio, testing the entire population may be practical.

## Test Attributes

For a trade, test whether:

- Decision approval existed
- Liquidity gate was completed
- Security was verified
- Order matched approval
- confirmation was reviewed
- settlement was completed
- cost basis was recorded
- account was reconciled

## Control Result

Use one result:

- Effective
- Effective with minor improvement
- Partially effective
- Ineffective
- Not performed
- Not applicable
- Unable to verify

## Control Deficiency

A deficiency exists when a control:

- Is missing
- Is poorly designed
- Was not performed
- Was performed late
- lacks evidence
- failed to detect a problem
- did not lead to correction

## Deficiency Severity

### Low

Limited effect and low likelihood of material loss.

### Moderate

Could create operational, reporting, or financial error.

### High

Could cause material financial, legal, tax, security, or property loss.

### Critical

Immediate exposure requiring transaction suspension or emergency action.

## Policy Compliance

Policy compliance testing compares actual actions with written requirements.

Possible requirements include:

- Maximum position size
- emergency reserve floor
- approved brokers
- permitted investments
- prohibited products
- allocation bands
- professional-review thresholds
- maximum property bid
- cooling-off period
- account-security requirements

## Policy Exception

A policy exception is a documented departure from an approved rule.

An exception should identify:

- Policy
- Requirement
- Proposed deviation
- Reason
- Risk
- Compensating control
- Approval
- Start date
- Expiration date
- Review date
- Final resolution

## Exception Versus Violation

### Approved exception

Authorized before or promptly after the deviation under a defined process.

### Policy violation

An unapproved or prohibited departure.

A repeated exception may indicate that:

- The policy is unrealistic
- Discipline is weak
- Risk tolerance has changed
- Approval is being misused

## Exception Criteria

An exception should not be approved merely because:

- The opportunity is attractive
- The transaction is urgent
- The investor dislikes the rule
- A prior exception was approved
- The market moved
- A salesperson applied pressure

## Compensating Control

A compensating control reduces risk when the standard control cannot be followed.

Example:

Standard requirement:

- Independent property inspection

Exception:

- Interior access is unavailable

Possible compensating controls:

- Larger repair contingency
- lower maximum bid
- external engineering review
- exterior inspection
- no-bid decision if uncertainty remains too high

A compensating control does not automatically make the risk acceptable.

## Exception Expiration

Every temporary exception should have an expiration date.

At expiration:

- Close the exception
- extend with new approval
- update the policy
- stop the affected activity

## Issue

An issue is a problem requiring correction.

Examples:

- Missing cost basis
- unreconciled account difference
- expired insurance
- beneficiary inconsistency
- unsupported property valuation
- missed backup test
- allocation limit breach
- undocumented professional advice
- unverified account access

## Issue Register

Record:

- Issue
- Source
- Date identified
- Risk
- Severity
- Owner
- Corrective action
- Target date
- Evidence required
- Status
- Closure approval

## Root Cause

Root-cause analysis identifies why the issue occurred.

Possible causes include:

- Missing procedure
- unclear responsibility
- training gap
- poor system design
- time pressure
- behavioral bias
- outdated policy
- inadequate evidence
- vendor failure
- deliberate noncompliance

## Five Whys

The Five Whys technique repeatedly asks why the problem occurred.

Example:

Problem:

- A trade was not reconciled.

Why?

- The monthly review was missed.

Why?

- No calendar reminder existed.

Why?

- Responsibility was not assigned.

Why?

- The governance plan did not identify an owner.

Root cause:

- Undefined responsibility and scheduling.

## Corrective Action

Corrective action should:

- Address root cause
- assign an owner
- define a deadline
- specify required evidence
- be proportionate
- be verified after completion

## Preventive Action

Preventive action reduces recurrence.

Examples:

- Add calendar automation
- simplify checklist
- require second-person review
- create account-reconciliation template
- update policy
- improve training
- restrict account permissions

## Issue Status

Use:

- Open
- Analysis in progress
- Remediation planned
- Remediation in progress
- Awaiting evidence
- Ready for closure
- Closed
- Risk accepted
- Overdue

## Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means consciously retaining a known risk.

It should document:

- Risk
- potential loss
- reason
- alternatives considered
- reserve
- approval
- expiration or review date

Risk acceptance should not conceal an unresolved problem.

## Overdue Issue

An overdue issue should trigger:

- Status escalation
- revised risk assessment
- investment or transaction pause where material
- new completion date
- explanation
- reviewer notification

## Annual Control Assessment

An annual assessment should answer:

- Were key controls performed?
- Did policies remain appropriate?
- Were limits respected?
- Were exceptions temporary?
- Were issues corrected?
- Were accounts reconciled?
- Were backups tested?
- Were beneficiaries reviewed?
- Was professional advice current?
- Were financial records complete?

## Annual Governance Report

A governance report may include:

## Governance Status

### Green

- Key controls effective
- no critical overdue issues
- accounts reconciled
- policies current
- exceptions controlled
- evidence complete

### Yellow

- Minor or moderate deficiencies
- incomplete evidence
- overdue noncritical items
- policy update required
- remediation underway

### Red

- Critical control failure
- unreconciled material balance
- suspected fraud
- missing professional review
- emergency reserve breach
- unauthorized transaction
- unresolved property-title risk
- critical backup or access failure

## Annual Certification

The decision owner may certify that:

- Material accounts were reviewed
- Policies were considered
- Known exceptions were disclosed
- Material issues were recorded
- No intentional information was omitted
- Required professional reviews were identified

Certification does not guarantee that every record is error free.

## Control Completion Rate

Control completion rate equals:

Controls completed on time
divided by controls scheduled

A high completion rate does not prove controls were performed well.

## Exception Aging

Track how long exceptions remain open.

Possible categories:

- 0 to 30 days
- 31 to 60 days
- 61 to 90 days
- More than 90 days

Long-lived exceptions may indicate hidden policy changes.

## Issue Aging

Track issues by:

- Severity

- owner

## Materiality

Materiality considers whether an issue could influence a significant financial decision.

Possible factors include:

- Amount
- percentage of net worth
- legal effect
- tax effect
- security effect
- property ownership
- household well-being
- recurrence

A small monetary error may still be material when it indicates fraud or control failure.

## Control Testing Priorities

Test first:

- Large transactions
- Irreversible transactions
- New institutions
- Property purchases
- cross-border transfers
- high-risk products
- prior control failures
- expired exceptions
- account-security changes

## Automatic Governance Stops

Stop new material investment actions when:

- Accounts are materially unreconciled
- Emergency reserve is below the approved floor
- Critical issue remains open
- Required professional review is missing
- Account security is compromised
- Policy exception expired
- property title remains unresolved
- tax filing exposure is unknown
- backup and emergency-access plans failed
- current decision records are materially incomplete

## Beginner Rules

1. Assign responsibility for every key control.
2. Keep policies, procedures, and records separate.
3. Test whether controls actually operate.
4. Retain evidence.
5. Document every material exception.
6. Give temporary exceptions expiration dates.
7. Correct root causes rather than symptoms.
8. Escalate overdue high-risk issues.
9. Review governance annually.
10. Stop new transactions when critical controls fail.
