# Lesson 42 — Financial Goals, Funding Priorities, and Capital-Allocation Roadmap

## Learning Objective

Learn how to define financial goals, estimate their total cost, assign deadlines and currencies, rank competing priorities, select suitable funding sources, measure progress, and prevent the same capital from being committed to multiple objectives.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

Investment suitability, tax consequences, pension access, insurance needs, property financing, and cross-border planning depend on personal circumstances and applicable law.

Material decisions should be reviewed with qualified professionals where appropriate.

## Core Principle

An investment is not a goal.

An ETF, CDT, savings account, property, or insurance policy is a financial tool.

The goal defines:

- Why the money is needed
- When it will be needed
- In which currency
- How much is required
- How certain the obligation is
- How much loss can be tolerated
- Which funding source is appropriate

## Goal Versus Obligation

### Goal

A desired future outcome.

Example:

- Purchase a vehicle within three years

### Obligation

A payment or responsibility that must be met.

Example:

- Property tax due next quarter

Obligations should generally receive priority over optional goals.

## SMART Financial Goal

A useful financial goal should be:

- Specific
- Measurable
- Achievable
- Relevant
- Time-bound

Example:

Weak goal:

- Save more for a vehicle

Stronger goal:

- Accumulate COP 35 million for a vehicle down payment by December 2028 without using the emergency reserve.

## Goal Priority

Possible priority levels:

### Critical

Failure could threaten:

- Health
- housing
- legal compliance
- essential income
- family welfare

### High

Important to household stability or a committed obligation.

### Medium

Valuable but can be adjusted or delayed.

### Low

Optional or aspirational.

## Priority Questions

Ask:

- What happens if this goal is not funded?
- Is the deadline legally fixed?
- Is another person dependent on it?
- Can the amount be reduced?
- Can the deadline move?
- Is borrowing available?
- Would failure threaten health or housing?
- Does another goal depend on completion?
- Is the goal reversible?
- Is the goal essential or discretionary?

## Goal Dependencies

Some goals depend on others.

Example:

Property purchase may require:

1. Emergency reserve
2. Debt affordability
3. Down payment
4. Closing costs
5. Repair reserve
6. Ownership reserve
7. Insurance
8. Legal review

The down payment alone does not establish readiness.

## Funding Hierarchy

A conservative funding order may be:

1. Essential current expenses
2. Minimum debt obligations
3. Emergency reserve
4. Medical and insurance reserves
5. Tax obligations
6. Legally or contractually committed goals
7. Near-term household goals
8. Retirement stability
9. Long-term diversified investing
10. Optional opportunity or lifestyle goals

## Goal Time Horizons

### Immediate

Needed within twelve months.

### Short term

Needed within approximately one to three years.

### Medium term

Needed within approximately three to seven years.

### Long term

Needed beyond seven years.

The exact definitions may be adjusted, but they should remain consistent.

## Time Horizon and Risk

Money needed soon should generally have limited exposure to:

- Equity-market decline
- long maturity
- property-sale delay
- legal uncertainty
- currency mismatch
- volatile instruments
- withdrawal restrictions

Longer horizons may allow more investment variability, subject to the goal’s importance and flexibility.

Classify how certain the funding must be.

### Very high certainty

Examples:

- Taxes
- essential medical care
- closing obligations
- emergency reserve
- required debt payment

### High certainty

Examples:

- Vehicle replacement
- dependent education

### Moderate certainty

Examples:

- Property investment
- optional renovation
- retirement enhancement

### Flexible

Examples:

- discretionary travel
- optional purchase
- opportunity fund

## Goal Flexibility

Record whether the goal allows changes to:

- Amount
- deadline
- quality
- location
- scope
- funding method

A flexible goal may tolerate more investment risk than an inflexible obligation.

## Goal Currency

Assign the currency in which the future expense will likely occur.

Possible currencies include:

- COP
- USD
- EUR
- Other

Examples:

- Colombian vehicle purchase: primarily COP
- US tax payment: potentially USD
- Colombian housing costs: primarily COP
- International travel: mixed currencies

## Currency-Matching Principle

Where practical, near-term goal assets should be aligned with the spending currency.

This can reduce the risk that exchange-rate movement creates a funding shortage.

## Multi-Currency Goal

For a mixed-currency goal, estimate each component separately.

Example:

International trip:

- Airfare in USD
- Hotel in destination currency
- Local transport
- COP preparation expenses

## Present Cost

Present cost is the estimated price if the goal were funded today.

Use current evidence such as:

- Written quote
- market comparison
- professional estimate
- official fee schedule
- historical spending
- current contract

## Future Cost

Future cost estimates how much may be required at the target date.

Educational formula:

Future cost equals:

Current cost
multiplied by
(1 + expected annual cost increase)
raised to the number of years

## Inflation Assumption

Use an assumption appropriate to the goal.

Different costs may rise at different rates.

Examples:

- Medical care
- education
- property
- construction
- vehicles
- travel
- general household expenses

Label assumptions clearly.

## Funding Gap

Funding gap equals:

Target future amount
minus current dedicated funding

Do not count money assigned to another goal.

## Monthly Contribution Requirement

A simplified calculation is:

Funding gap
divided by months remaining

This ignores investment return.

A more detailed calculation may include:

- Expected contributions
- expected return
- fees
- taxes
- currency
- timing of deposits

Use conservative assumptions.

## Return Assumption

Expected return should not be used to make an unaffordable goal appear achievable.

For critical short-term goals, it may be safer to assume:

- Little or no investment return
- known interest only
- fees
- tax
- currency uncertainty

## Contribution Sources

Possible funding sources include:

- Pension surplus
- salary
- consulting income
- debt-payment savings
- investment distributions
- property-sale proceeds
- CDT maturity
- bonus
- inheritance
- business income
- reduced discretionary spending

Do not count uncertain income as committed funding.

## Dedicated Funding

Dedicated funding is money assigned exclusively to one goal.

Record:

- Account or bucket
- current value
- currency
- liquidity
- restrictions
- ownership
- expected maturity
- permitted use

## Shared Funding Pool

A shared pool may support multiple flexible goals, but the total planned allocation cannot exceed the pool.

Maintain:

- Total available amount
- provisional allocations
- priority order
- decision deadline
- release conditions

## Double-Commitment Risk

Double commitment occurs when the same money is counted toward multiple goals.

Example:

COP 30 million is simultaneously counted as:

- Emergency reserve
- Vehicle down payment
- Property opportunity fund

Only one use can occur at a time.

## Goal Funding Ratio

Goal funding ratio equals:

Current dedicated funding
divided by target amount

Example:

- Target: COP 50 million
- Dedicated funding: COP 20 million
- Funding ratio: 40%

## Goal Status

Possible status levels:

### Green

- Funding plan is on schedule
- dedicated assets are suitable
- no material dependency unresolved

### Yellow

- Contribution gap
- deadline pressure
- currency mismatch
- uncertain cost
- dependency incomplete

### Red

- Required obligation unfunded
- deadline cannot be met
- emergency reserve would be used
- investment risk is inconsistent with deadline
- funding counted more than once
- goal depends on unconfirmed income

## Goal Feasibility

A goal is financially feasible when:

- Target cost is supportable
- contribution requirement fits cash flow
- other priorities remain funded
- required reserves remain intact
- risk is appropriate
- deadline is realistic
- currency needs are addressed
- debt remains manageable

## Goal Trade-Offs

When goals compete, possible responses include:

- Increase contribution
- extend deadline
- reduce scope
- reduce target amount
- change quality or location
- delay lower-priority goal
- sell an unneeded asset
- reduce discretionary spending
- increase reliable income
- avoid additional debt

## Opportunity Cost

Funding one goal may reduce progress toward another.

Example:

Additional vehicle down payment may reduce:

- Investment contribution
- property reserve
- debt payoff
- travel fund
- liquidity

Every material allocation decision should identify the displaced alternative.

## Goal Conflict

A goal conflict exists when two objectives compete for:

- The same money
- The same monthly surplus
- The same borrowing capacity
- The same currency
- The same deadline
- The same property
- The same risk tolerance

## Goal-Conflict Resolution

1. Identify the conflict.
2. classify each priority.
3. identify mandatory obligations.
4. compare consequences of delay.
5. measure affordability.
6. protect critical reserves.
7. select the trade-off.
8. document the decision.
9. update affected goals.
10. schedule review.

A goal is complete when:

- Required amount is available
- payment or objective is completed
- final costs are reconciled
- unused funds are reassigned

## Goal Funding Decision Gate

Before committing money, confirm:

- Goal is documented
- priority is approved
- target cost is current
- currency is correct
- deadline is realistic
- funding is dedicated
- reserves remain intact
- debt remains manageable
- risk matches time horizon
- no double commitment exists
- affected goals were reviewed

## Scenario Planning

## Scenario 1 — Income Declines

Recalculate:

- Monthly contributions
- completion date
- lower-priority goals
- required spending reductions

## Scenario 2 — Goal Cost Increases

Increase the target cost.

Determine:

- New funding gap
- contribution increase
- deadline change
- scope reduction

## Scenario 3 — COP Strengthens

For a goal funded in USD but payable in COP, model the effect on COP value.

## Scenario 4 — COP Weakens

For a USD obligation funded in COP, calculate the additional COP required.

## Scenario 5 — Investment Declines

Reduce goal assets exposed to markets.

Determine whether:

- Deadline remains achievable
- contributions must rise
- risk must fall
- goal must be delayed

## Scenario 6 — Medical Emergency

Redirect available surplus and review:

- Optional goals
- emergency reserve
- insurance
- investment contributions

## Scenario 7 — Two Goals Occur Together

Assume:

- Vehicle replacement
- property repair
- tax payment

Determine which obligations can be funded and which must be delayed.

## Scenario 8 — Expected Job Does Not Begin

Remove unconfirmed employment income from the plan.

Recalculate all dependent goals.

## Goal Portfolio

A goal portfolio shows all objectives together.

It should identify:

- Total target funding

- overlapping deadlines
- currency requirements
- critical funding gaps
- dependent goals

## Capital-Allocation Roadmap

A roadmap converts priorities into planned funding phases.

Example:

### Phase 1

- Complete emergency reserve
- fund tax reserve
- stabilize high-interest debt

### Phase 2

- Fund vehicle or property down payment
- maintain insurance and medical reserves
- continue minimum long-term investing

### Phase 3

- Increase retirement investment
- establish opportunity reserve
- fund optional travel

## Funding Phase Entry Criteria

Do not advance to the next phase until defined conditions are met.

Examples:

- Emergency reserve funded
- no delinquent debt
- tax reserve current
- insurance active
- required documents complete
- monthly surplus verified

## Funding Phase Exit Criteria

A phase is complete when:

- Target reached
- obligation paid
- risk resolved
- goal cancelled
- capital reassigned

## Goal Concentration

A household may concentrate too much future capital into one objective.

Examples:

- Most savings allocated to one property
- all future surplus assigned to one vehicle
- no retirement investment while funding discretionary goals
- excessive education commitment without emergency protection

## Goal-Risk Budget

Define how much uncertainty each goal can tolerate.

Possible categories:

- No material loss acceptable
- Small temporary variation acceptable
- Moderate variation acceptable
- High variation acceptable because deadline is flexible

## Behavioral Risks

Goal planning can be distorted by:

- Optimism
- lifestyle comparison
- urgency
- anchoring on asking price
- sunk costs
- fear of missing out
- expected job income
- expected inheritance
- unsupported property appreciation

## Stop Conditions

Pause or reject goal funding when:

- Required amount is unknown
- Deadline is unclear
- Funding is counted twice
- Emergency reserves would be reduced below policy
- Goal depends on unconfirmed income
- currency mismatch is untested
- short-term obligation uses volatile assets
- debt affordability is incomplete
- higher-priority obligation is unfunded
- material professional review is missing
- target cost is based only on promotional claims
- the household cannot explain what will be delayed

## Beginner Rules

1. Define the goal before selecting the investment.
2. Separate obligations from optional goals.
3. Assign one primary purpose to each funding balance.
4. Match risk to deadline and certainty.
5. Match near-term funding to spending currency.
6. Protect emergency, medical, and tax reserves.
7. Do not rely on expected income before it begins.
8. Document trade-offs between competing goals.
9. Review all goals together.
10. Update the roadmap after major life changes.
