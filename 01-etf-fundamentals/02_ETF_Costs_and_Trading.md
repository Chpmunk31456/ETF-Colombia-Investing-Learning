# Lesson 2 — ETF Costs and Trading Mechanics

## Learning Objective

Understand the main direct and indirect costs of owning an ETF and how trading mechanics can affect the actual price paid.

## Why Costs Matter

Investment returns are uncertain, but costs are real.

Even a small annual fee can reduce long-term returns because the money paid in fees no longer compounds for the investor.

ETF costs are not limited to the expense ratio. A complete review should include:

- Expense ratio
- Brokerage commission
- Bid-ask spread
- Currency-conversion cost
- Custody or platform fees
- Tax costs
- Tracking difference
- Possible withdrawal or transfer fees

## Expense Ratio

The expense ratio is the annual percentage of fund assets used to pay the ETF's operating expenses.

Example:

If an ETF has an expense ratio of 0.20%, the annual fund cost is approximately:

- USD 2 for every USD 1,000 invested
- USD 20 for every USD 10,000 invested
- USD 200 for every USD 100,000 invested

The fee is normally deducted inside the fund. It is not usually charged as a separate invoice.

## Expense Ratio Is Not the Total Cost

Two ETFs can have the same expense ratio but different total investor costs.

Possible differences include:

- Wider or narrower bid-ask spreads
- Better or worse tracking
- Different brokerage commissions
- Different tax treatment
- Different currency-conversion fees
- Different liquidity
- Different withholding-tax exposure

## Bid and Ask Prices

### Bid

The bid is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.

### Ask

The ask is the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.

### Spread

The bid-ask spread is the difference between those two prices.

Example:

- Bid: USD 99.95
- Ask: USD 100.05
- Spread: USD 0.10

A narrower spread is generally better for the investor because less value is lost when entering or exiting a position.

## Market Orders

A market order instructs the broker to execute the trade at the best price currently available.

Advantages:

- Usually executes quickly
- Simple to use

Risks:

- Final execution price may differ from the displayed price
- Can be costly in thinly traded ETFs
- Can produce unfavorable results during volatile markets
- Can be risky near market opening or closing

## Limit Orders

A limit order sets the maximum purchase price or minimum sale price the investor will accept.

Example:

An investor may place a buy limit order at USD 100.00.

The order will execute only if shares are available at USD 100.00 or lower.

Advantages:

- More control over execution price
- Helps reduce unexpected price slippage

Risks:

- The order may not execute
- A partially filled order may occur
- The market can move away from the limit price

## Liquidity

ETF liquidity can be evaluated through:

- Average daily trading volume
- Bid-ask spread
- Assets under management
- Liquidity of the underlying securities
- Number and quality of market makers

High trading volume can be helpful, but the liquidity of the underlying assets also matters.

A bond ETF or international ETF may have lower visible trading volume while still having meaningful underlying liquidity.

## Tracking Difference

Tracking difference is the difference between the ETF's actual return and the return of its benchmark.

An ETF may trail its benchmark because of:

- Expense ratio
- Trading costs
- Taxes
- Sampling methods
- Cash holdings
- Rebalancing delays
- Securities-lending practices

A lower expense ratio does not always guarantee better benchmark tracking.

## Currency-Conversion Costs

An investor living in Colombia may earn or hold money in Colombian pesos but buy an ETF priced in US dollars.

Potential costs include:

- COP-to-USD conversion spread
- Bank transfer fees
- Brokerage conversion fees
- International wire fees
- Withdrawal conversion costs

These costs should be measured before investing.

## Platform and Custody Fees

A broker may charge:

- Monthly account fees
- Inactivity fees
- Custody fees
- Data fees
- Withdrawal fees
- Foreign-market access fees
- Transfer-out fees

A "commission-free" trade does not necessarily mean the account is free.

## Taxes and Withholding

Possible tax-related costs may include:

- Dividend withholding
- Capital-gains tax
- Colombian tax reporting
- US tax reporting
- Foreign tax-credit limitations
- Estate-tax considerations
- PFIC reporting for certain non-US pooled funds

Tax treatment can materially change the real return.

The applicable treatment depends on factors including citizenship, tax residence, account jurisdiction, fund domicile, asset type, holding period, and applicable tax treaties or credits. This lesson identifies questions for professional review; it does not determine an investor’s tax liability.

## Beginner Trading Rules

1. Understand the ETF before placing an order.
2. Check the bid and ask prices.
3. Prefer limit orders when price control matters.
4. Avoid trading during unusually volatile conditions.
5. Avoid buying immediately at market open without understanding the risks.
6. Confirm the correct ticker and exchange.
7. Review currency-conversion costs.
8. Record the actual execution price.
9. Keep official confirmations and tax records.
10. Never assume "commission-free" means cost-free.
