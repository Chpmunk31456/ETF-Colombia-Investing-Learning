# Lesson 57 — ETF Securities Lending, Derivatives, Counterparty, and Collateral Risk

## Learning Objective

Learn how exchange-traded funds may use securities lending, swaps, futures, forwards, options, repurchase agreements, collateral, and counterparties; how these activities can affect tracking, income, liquidity, tax, and loss exposure; and how to review official disclosures before investing.

## Important Disclaimer

This lesson is educational.

Fund structures, collateral requirements, derivatives rules, counterparty limits, tax treatment, securities-lending arrangements, and regulatory protections vary by jurisdiction and can change.

Use current official fund documents and qualified investment, legal, and tax professionals for material decisions.

## Core Principle

An ETF may appear simple while relying on operational arrangements that are not obvious from its ticker or holdings summary.

Important hidden or less-visible exposures may include:

- Securities lending
- derivatives
- counterparty credit
- collateral quality
- collateral valuation
- leverage
- liquidity
- settlement
- rehypothecation
- indemnification limitations
- tax treatment
- operational dependency

## Securities Lending

Securities lending occurs when a fund temporarily lends portfolio securities to an approved borrower.

The borrower generally provides collateral and pays a lending fee.

## Why Funds Lend Securities

Possible objectives include:

- Generate additional revenue
- offset operating expenses
- improve tracking difference
- support market liquidity
- facilitate short selling
- support settlement activity

## Borrower

A borrower may be:

- Broker-dealer
- investment bank
- market maker
- financial institution
- other approved counterparty

## Lending Agent

A lending agent administers the securities-lending program.

Possible responsibilities include:

- Borrower selection
- collateral collection
- collateral valuation
- margin calls
- income allocation
- transaction processing
- default response

The lending agent may be affiliated with the fund sponsor or custodian.

## Affiliated Lending Agent

An affiliated lending agent may create conflicts involving:

- Borrower selection
- fee sharing
- collateral investment
- revenue allocation
- risk tolerance

Review fund disclosures and governance controls.

## Lending Revenue

Gross lending revenue is the total income generated before deductions.

Net lending revenue is the amount retained by the fund after:

- Lending-agent fee
- operational cost
- borrower rebates
- collateral-management cost
- other deductions

## Revenue Split

The revenue split determines how lending income is allocated among:

- Fund
- lending agent
- custodian
- manager
- other service providers

## Lending Revenue Benefit

Lending revenue may reduce the effective cost of holding the ETF.

It does not eliminate:

- Counterparty risk
- collateral risk
- operational risk
- market risk
- fund expenses

## Securities on Loan

The percentage of portfolio securities on loan indicates the extent of lending activity.

A high percentage may increase:

- Lending revenue
- borrower exposure
- collateral exposure
- operational complexity

## Borrower Default

Borrower default occurs when the borrower fails to return lent securities or meet contractual obligations.

Possible causes include:

- Insolvency
- liquidity crisis
- operational failure
- fraud
- market disruption
- settlement failure

## Collateral

Collateral is property pledged to reduce exposure to the borrower.

Possible collateral includes:

- Cash
- government securities
- high-quality bonds
- equities
- other permitted instruments

## Collateral Value

Collateral value should generally exceed the value of securities lent under applicable program rules.

## Overcollateralization

Overcollateralization means collateral exceeds the market value of the lent securities.

Example:

Securities lent:

USD 100 million

Collateral received:

USD 102 million

Collateralization level:

102%

## Collateralization Formula

Collateralization percentage equals:

Collateral market value
divided by
market value of securities lent

multiplied by 100

## Daily Mark-to-Market

Securities and collateral may be revalued daily or more frequently.

When collateral falls below the required level, the borrower may be required to provide additional collateral.

## Margin Call

A margin call requires additional collateral after exposure changes.

## Collateral Gap Risk

A collateral gap can occur when:

- Borrower defaults
- collateral value falls
- lent security value rises
- liquidation is delayed
- markets become illiquid

## Collateral Quality

Review:

- Asset type
- issuer
- credit quality
- maturity
- liquidity
- currency
- concentration
- correlation with borrower
- correlation with lent securities

## Wrong-Way Risk

Wrong-way risk occurs when collateral or counterparty quality deteriorates at the same time exposure increases.

Example:

A financial institution borrows financial-sector shares and posts collateral that is also highly exposed to financial-sector stress.

## Cash Collateral

Cash collateral may be reinvested.

Reinvestment may introduce:

- Credit risk
- interest-rate risk
- liquidity risk
- maturity mismatch
- market-value loss
- spread risk

## Non-Cash Collateral

Non-cash collateral may include securities held under the lending agreement.

Review whether the fund:

- Owns the collateral
- has a security interest
- can liquidate it
- faces custody restrictions
- faces jurisdictional limits

## Collateral Reinvestment

Cash collateral may be invested in:

- Money-market instruments
- short-term debt
- repurchase agreements
- pooled collateral funds
- bank deposits

Collateral reinvestment can generate income but may also create loss.

## Indemnification

A lending agent or custodian may provide indemnification against specified borrower-default losses.

Indemnification may not cover:

- Collateral reinvestment loss
- operational error
- legal dispute
- market loss
- every borrower
- every jurisdiction
- every event

## Indemnification Provider Risk

Indemnification is only as reliable as:

- Provider financial strength
- contract wording
- exclusions
- legal enforceability
- available capital

## Recall of Lent Securities

The fund may recall lent securities for:

- Sale
- rebalancing
- redemption
- voting
- corporate action

Recall may be delayed or unsuccessful during stress.

## Voting Rights

When securities are on loan, voting rights may temporarily transfer to the borrower.

Review the fund’s policy for recalling securities before important votes.

## Dividend Equivalent Payment

A borrower may make a substitute or equivalent payment when a dividend occurs while securities are on loan.

Tax treatment may differ from an ordinary dividend.

## Short Selling Relationship

Borrowed securities may support short selling.

Securities lending by a fund does not mean the fund itself is short the security.

## Lending Concentration

Review concentration by:

- Borrower
- lending agent
- collateral issuer
- collateral type
- currency
- market

## Counterparty

A counterparty is another party to a financial contract.

ETF counterparties may include:

- Swap dealer
- futures clearing broker
- forward dealer
- options dealer
- repurchase-agreement counterparty
- securities borrower
- custodian
- subcustodian

## Counterparty Credit Risk

Counterparty credit risk is the possibility that a counterparty fails to perform.

## Gross Exposure

Gross exposure is the amount before considering collateral or netting.

## Net Exposure

Net exposure may reflect:

- Collateral
- contractual netting
- offsetting positions
- margin

Net exposure depends on legal enforceability.

## Netting Agreement

A netting agreement may allow obligations to be combined after default or termination.

Its effectiveness depends on:

- Contract
- jurisdiction
- insolvency law
- counterparty
- legal opinion

## Derivative

A derivative is a financial contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, rate, index, currency, or event.

## Common ETF Derivatives

ETFs may use:

- Futures
- swaps
- forwards
- options
- total-return swaps
- credit-default swaps
- repurchase agreements

## Futures Contract

A futures contract creates an obligation to buy or sell exposure under standardized exchange rules at a future date.

Possible uses include:

- Efficient market exposure
- cash equitization
- duration management
- commodity exposure
- hedging

## Futures Margin

Futures typically require margin rather than full upfront payment.

This creates economic leverage.

## Variation Margin

Variation margin reflects daily gains and losses on futures positions.

## Futures Basis Risk

Basis risk occurs when futures performance differs from the target exposure.

## Futures Roll

A futures contract may need to be replaced before expiration.

This is called rolling.

## Roll Yield

Roll yield can be positive or negative depending on futures prices across maturities.

## Contango

Contango generally exists when longer-dated futures prices exceed nearer-dated prices.

Repeated rolling in contango may create drag.

## Backwardation

Backwardation generally exists when longer-dated futures prices are below nearer-dated prices.

Rolling may create a benefit, but results are not guaranteed.

## Swap

A swap is a contract exchanging defined financial payments.

## Total-Return Swap

A total-return swap may provide the return of an index or asset in exchange for:

- Financing payment
- fixed payment
- floating payment
- another return stream

## Synthetic ETF

A synthetic ETF may use swaps to obtain benchmark exposure rather than holding every benchmark security directly.

## Unfunded Swap Structure

In an unfunded structure, the fund may own a substitute portfolio and enter into a swap for the difference between:

- Substitute portfolio return
- target index return

## Funded Swap Structure

In a funded structure, investor cash may be transferred under the swap structure, with collateral held separately under stated arrangements.

## Substitute Basket

A substitute basket is a portfolio held by the fund that may differ from the target index.

Review:

- Securities
- liquidity
- valuation
- concentration
- eligibility
- collateral relationship

## Swap Counterparty Exposure

Review:

- Counterparty name
- exposure limit
- collateral
- reset frequency
- diversification
- legal agreement
- termination rights

## Swap Reset

A swap reset reduces accumulated counterparty exposure by settling amounts periodically.

## Counterparty Limit

Regulation or fund policy may limit net exposure to one counterparty.

Confirm the actual method used.

## Multiple Counterparties

Using several counterparties may reduce concentration but increase:

- Operational complexity
- documentation
- valuation differences
- settlement dependencies

## Forward Contract

A forward is a privately negotiated contract to exchange an asset or currency later at agreed terms.

ETFs may use forwards for:

- Currency hedging
- exposure management
- settlement

## Currency Forward Risk

Currency forwards introduce:

- Counterparty risk
- rollover cost
- basis risk
- collateral requirements
- imperfect hedge
- settlement risk

## Option

An option provides a contractual right or obligation based on specified terms.

Options may create nonlinear risk.

## Purchased Option

A purchased option generally limits direct premium loss to the premium paid, subject to other portfolio effects.

## Written Option

A written option can create substantial obligations.

Review whether the fund uses:

- Covered calls
- put writing
- index options
- protective options
- option spreads

## Covered-Call ETF

A covered-call ETF may hold securities and sell call options.

Possible effects include:

- Option income
- reduced upside participation
- downside exposure remains
- tax complexity
- path-dependent results

High distribution yield does not mean high total return.

## Put-Writing Strategy

A put-writing strategy may receive option premium but can experience substantial losses when the underlying market declines.

## Repurchase Agreement

A repurchase agreement involves the sale of securities with an agreement to repurchase them later.

It may function economically like secured borrowing or lending.

## Reverse Repurchase Agreement

A reverse repurchase agreement is the opposite side of the transaction from the fund’s perspective.

## Repo Counterparty Risk

Repo risk may involve:

- Counterparty default
- collateral decline
- settlement failure
- legal enforceability
- maturity mismatch

## Central Clearing

Some derivatives are centrally cleared.

A clearinghouse becomes part of the transaction chain.

Central clearing may reduce bilateral counterparty exposure but introduces:

- Clearinghouse dependency
- clearing-broker dependency
- margin requirements
- default-fund exposure
- operational concentration

## Bilateral Derivative

A bilateral derivative is directly contracted between two counterparties.

## Clearing Broker

A clearing broker facilitates access to a clearinghouse.

Failure of the clearing broker can create operational and access risk.

## Collateral Segregation

Segregation separates collateral from the counterparty’s own assets under defined arrangements.

Review:

- Legal ownership
- custody
- rehypothecation
- insolvency treatment
- account structure

## Rehypothecation

Rehypothecation occurs when collateral received is reused or pledged again where permitted.

It can increase:

- Interconnectedness
- recovery complexity
- counterparty dependency

## Initial Margin

Initial margin is collateral posted when a derivative position is opened.

## Variation Margin

Variation margin adjusts collateral as market value changes.

## Haircut

A haircut reduces the recognized value of collateral.

Example:

Collateral market value:

USD 100

Recognized collateral value after a 5% haircut:

USD 95

## Collateral Haircut Formula

Recognized collateral value equals:

Market value
multiplied by
(1 - haircut percentage)

## Leverage

Leverage increases economic exposure relative to invested capital or net assets.

## Gross Leverage

Gross leverage may sum absolute exposures without netting.

## Net Leverage

Net leverage may reflect offsets or hedges.

The formula varies by fund and regulation.

## Embedded Leverage

Embedded leverage can arise through:

- Futures
- swaps
- written options
- leveraged loans
- structured notes
- reverse repos

## Leverage Effect

Leverage magnifies:

- Gains
- losses
- volatility
- margin needs
- liquidation risk
- path dependency

## Daily Reset

Leveraged and inverse ETFs often target a multiple of daily benchmark performance.

They do not promise the same multiple over longer periods.

## Compounding Effect

Compounding can cause long-term returns to differ materially from the daily target multiple.

## Volatility Decay

In volatile markets, repeated daily reset may reduce value even when the benchmark finishes near its starting point.

## Path Dependency

Path dependency means the sequence of returns affects the final result.

## Counterparty Default Scenario

A default assessment should ask:

1. What is the gross exposure?
2. what collateral exists?
3. where is collateral held?
4. how often is it valued?
5. what haircut applies?
6. can positions be closed?
7. how liquid is the collateral?
8. what does indemnification cover?
9. which jurisdiction controls?
10. what losses remain?

## Collateral Liquidation

After default, collateral may need to be sold.

Loss can arise from:

- Price decline
- delay
- transaction cost
- legal restriction
- market closure
- illiquidity
- currency mismatch

## Liquidity Mismatch

Liquidity mismatch occurs when a fund offers frequent trading while holding instruments that may be difficult to sell or close quickly.

## Derivative Liquidity

Derivative liquidity depends on:

- Market depth
- counterparties
- exchange
- collateral
- volatility
- contract maturity
- clearing
- market hours

## Valuation Risk

Derivatives may require models when market prices are unavailable.

Model valuation may depend on:

- Volatility
- interest rates
- credit spread
- correlation
- liquidity
- counterparty
- time to maturity

## Model Risk

Model risk occurs when valuation assumptions or methods are incorrect or inappropriate.

## Pricing Source

Review whether derivatives are valued using:

- Exchange settlement
- counterparty quote
- independent pricing service
- internal model
- administrator calculation

## Valuation Dispute

A fund and counterparty may disagree about contract value or collateral.

## Operational Risk

Operational risk includes:

- Incorrect trade
- settlement failure
- collateral error
- stale price
- documentation failure
- system outage
- reconciliation failure
- unauthorized transaction

## Legal Risk

Legal risk includes uncertainty about:

- Contract enforceability
- collateral ownership
- netting
- insolvency
- jurisdiction
- investor rights

## Documentation

Common derivative documentation may include:

- Master agreement
- collateral annex
- clearing agreement
- confirmations
- custody agreement
- lending agreement

Individual investors generally rely on fund disclosures rather than receiving these contracts directly.

## Prospectus Review

The prospectus may disclose:

- Permitted derivatives
- investment purpose
- leverage
- securities lending
- counterparty limits
- collateral
- valuation
- liquidity
- indemnification
- tax risks

## Annual Report Review

The annual report may provide:

- Derivative notional exposure
- counterparties
- collateral
- securities on loan
- lending revenue
- lending-agent fees
- realized and unrealized results
- repurchase agreements

## Statement of Investments

The statement of investments may identify:

- Derivative contracts
- counterparties
- notional values
- collateral
- securities on loan
- fair-value level

## Notional Amount

Notional amount is the reference amount used to calculate derivative payments.

Notional amount is not always the same as:

- Market value
- cash invested
- maximum loss
- net exposure

## Fair Value

Fair value is the accounting value assigned to a position under the applicable methodology.

## Derivative Assets and Liabilities

A derivative may appear as:

- Asset when favorable to the fund
- liability when unfavorable to the fund

## Fair-Value Hierarchy

Financial statements may classify valuations by levels based on observable market inputs.

A higher reliance on unobservable inputs may indicate greater valuation uncertainty.

## Counterparty Exposure Register

A review should record:

- Counterparty
- contract type
- gross exposure
- collateral
- net exposure
- maturity
- jurisdiction
- credit review
- concentration

## Securities-Lending Register

A review should record:

- Percentage on loan
- collateral type
- collateralization level
- lending revenue
- fund revenue share
- lending agent
- indemnification
- largest borrower exposure

## Derivative-Purpose Classification

Classify derivative use as:

- Hedging
- efficient portfolio management
- cash equitization
- index replication
- duration management
- currency management
- leverage
- income generation
- speculative exposure

## Hedging

Hedging seeks to reduce a defined risk.

A hedge can be:

- Partial
- imperfect
- expensive
- temporary
- ineffective under stress

## Efficient Portfolio Management

This phrase may describe derivative use for:

- Lower transaction cost
- liquidity
- index alignment
- cash management

Review the actual activity rather than relying on the label.

## Derivative Income

Option premium or swap income should not be evaluated separately from:

- Loss exposure
- foregone upside
- financing cost
- tax
- volatility
- total return

## Distribution Yield Warning

An ETF may distribute amounts generated by:

- Dividends
- interest
- option premium
- realized gain
- return of capital

A high distribution does not prove:

- High income quality
- capital preservation
- superior total return
- sustainable payout

## Return of Capital

A distribution classified as return of capital may reduce cost basis or represent return of the investor’s own capital under applicable rules.

Professional tax review may be required.

## Collateral Currency Risk

Collateral denominated in another currency may create additional exchange-rate exposure.

## Maturity Mismatch

Maturity mismatch occurs when:

- Short-term funding supports longer-term exposure
- collateral liquidity differs from obligation timing
- derivatives require renewal before investment horizon

## Rollover Risk

Rollover risk arises when expiring contracts must be replaced.

Replacement may occur at:

- Higher cost
- lower liquidity
- different pricing
- changed collateral terms

## Counterparty Correlation

A counterparty may be economically correlated with:

- Fund holdings
- collateral
- market sector
- country
- currency

This can increase stress losses.

## Custodian Risk

A custodian holds fund assets under defined legal arrangements.

Review:

- Asset segregation
- subcustodians
- jurisdiction
- insolvency treatment
- operational controls

## Subcustodian Risk

Global funds may rely on local subcustodians.

Risks may include:

- Local law
- settlement
- recordkeeping
- capital controls
- political risk
- operational failure

## Prime Broker

Some complex funds may use prime brokers for:

- Financing
- custody
- derivatives
- short selling
- settlement

Prime-broker concentration can create dependency.

## Counterparty Diversification

Counterparty diversification reduces reliance on one party but does not eliminate systemic risk.

## Systemic Risk

Systemic risk occurs when stress affects many institutions or markets simultaneously.

During systemic stress:

- Collateral may decline
- spreads may widen
- counterparties may weaken
- liquidity may disappear
- correlations may rise
- settlement may slow

## Stress Testing

A fund or investor review may consider:

- Counterparty default
- collateral decline
- market closure
- margin increase
- volatility spike
- derivative gap
- lending recall failure
- fund outflows
- currency shock

## Counterparty Exposure Limit

A personal investment policy may define restrictions such as:

- No synthetic ETF without review
- no leveraged ETF
- no inverse ETF
- no uncovered option strategy
- maximum allocation to complex products
- professional review above threshold

## Complexity Budget

A complexity budget limits how much of the portfolio may depend on structures that require specialized monitoring.

## Complexity Cost

Complexity can create:

- Professional expense
- misunderstanding
- monitoring burden
- tax complexity
- estate complexity
- execution risk
- documentation risk

## Simpler Alternative

Before selecting a complex ETF, compare:

- Broad physical ETF
- direct bond or deposit
- unhedged share class
- traditional income fund
- locally available regulated product

A simpler option may be preferable even when expected return is slightly lower.

## Risk Status

### Green

- Structure understood
- counterparty exposure limited
- collateral quality acceptable
- leverage within policy
- disclosures current
- complexity fits investor knowledge

### Yellow

- Counterparty concentration increased
- collateral detail incomplete
- derivative exposure increased
- lending level elevated
- professional review required

### Red

- Structure cannot be explained
- leverage is unclear
- collateral cannot be identified
- counterparty exposure is undisclosed
- prospectus is unavailable
- distribution source is misunderstood
- product exceeds policy
- material default or collateral event exists

## Automatic Stop Conditions

Do not purchase or retain a complex ETF without further review when:

- Derivative purpose is unclear
- synthetic structure is misunderstood
- counterparty identity is unavailable
- collateral policy is unclear
- leverage cannot be measured
- distribution is being mistaken for guaranteed income
- securities-lending revenue split is undisclosed
- indemnification is assumed but unverified
- collateral is illiquid or highly concentrated
- swap exposure exceeds policy
- strategy requires daily monitoring that will not occur
- tax treatment is unresolved
- simpler alternatives were not evaluated

## Beginner Rules

1. Read beyond the ETF name and holdings list.
2. identify securities lending and derivative use.
3. distinguish notional amount from net exposure.
4. review collateral quality and location.
5. identify counterparties and concentration.
6. do not assume indemnification eliminates risk.
7. understand leverage and daily reset.
8. evaluate total return rather than distribution yield alone.
9. compare complex products with simpler alternatives.
10. stop when the structure cannot be clearly explained.
