# Lesson 3 — Diversification, Risk, and Drawdowns

## Learning Objective

Understand what diversification can and cannot do, how ETF risk should be evaluated, and why temporary losses are a normal part of investing.

## What Diversification Means

Diversification means spreading investment exposure across multiple holdings, sectors, countries, issuers, or asset classes.

The purpose is to reduce the impact of one investment performing poorly.

Diversification does not guarantee a profit and does not prevent losses during a broad market decline.

## Diversification Within One ETF

An ETF may appear diversified because it owns many securities, but the number of holdings alone is not enough.

A fund with 500 holdings may still be concentrated if:

- Its largest companies dominate the portfolio
- Most holdings are in one country
- One sector represents a large percentage
- Holdings respond similarly to the same economic risks
- The index uses market-cap weighting
- Several companies are controlled by the same industry trends

## Diversification Across ETFs

Owning several ETFs does not automatically create more diversification.

Two ETFs may hold many of the same companies.

This is called portfolio overlap.

Example:

- ETF A tracks large US companies
- ETF B tracks a technology-heavy index
- ETF C tracks US growth companies

All three may have large positions in the same technology companies.

The investor may believe the portfolio is diversified while actually increasing concentration.

## Types of Diversification

### Company diversification

Exposure is spread among different companies.

### Sector diversification

Exposure is spread among industries such as:

- Technology
- Healthcare
- Financial services
- Consumer goods
- Energy
- Industrials
- Utilities

### Geographic diversification

Exposure is spread among:

- United States
- Developed international markets
- Emerging markets
- Colombia
- Other regions

### Asset-class diversification

Exposure is spread among:

- Stocks
- Bonds
- Cash
- Real estate
- Other assets

### Currency diversification

Exposure is spread among currencies such as:

- Colombian peso
- US dollar
- Euro
- Other currencies

Currency diversification can reduce dependence on one currency, but it also introduces exchange-rate risk.

## Market Risk

Market risk is the possibility that an entire market declines.

A diversified stock ETF can still lose substantial value during:

- Recessions
- Financial crises
- Wars
- Pandemics
- Interest-rate shocks
- Political instability
- Market bubbles
- Sudden changes in investor confidence

Diversification reduces some risks but does not eliminate market risk.

## Company-Specific Risk

Company-specific risk is the possibility that one business performs poorly because of:

- Management failure
- Fraud
- Product failure
- Debt problems
- Lawsuits
- Regulation
- Competition
- Technological disruption

A broad ETF usually reduces this risk because no single company represents the entire investment.

## Sector Risk

Sector risk occurs when a particular industry performs poorly.

Examples include:

- Technology valuations falling
- Energy prices collapsing
- Banks suffering credit losses
- Healthcare firms facing regulation
- Real-estate securities declining when rates rise

A sector ETF may be diversified among companies but still concentrated in one economic risk.

## Country Risk

Country risk includes:

- Political instability
- Currency controls
- Tax changes
- Regulatory changes
- Economic recession
- Sovereign debt problems
- Restrictions on capital movement

A Colombia-focused investment may be exposed to Colombian economic and currency risks.

A foreign ETF may reduce country concentration but introduce other legal, tax, and currency risks.

## Currency Risk

Currency risk occurs when an investment and the investor's spending needs use different currencies.

Example:

- The ETF is priced in US dollars
- Household expenses are paid in Colombian pesos

The investment return measured in pesos depends on:

1. The ETF's market return
2. The change in the USD/COP exchange rate
3. Currency-conversion costs

A rising dollar can increase the peso value of a US investment.

A falling dollar can reduce the peso value, even when the ETF itself gains in dollars.

## Interest-Rate Risk

Interest-rate risk is especially important for bond ETFs.

When market interest rates rise, existing bond prices often fall.

Long-duration bond ETFs are generally more sensitive to interest-rate changes than short-duration bond ETFs.

## Credit Risk

Credit risk is the possibility that a bond issuer cannot make scheduled interest or principal payments.

Government bond ETFs and corporate bond ETFs can have different levels of credit risk.

Higher yield usually comes with higher risk.

## Volatility

Volatility measures how much an investment's price moves over time.

High volatility means prices may rise or fall sharply.

Low volatility does not mean no risk.

An investment may appear stable for years and still suffer a large decline under unusual conditions.

## Drawdown

A drawdown is the decline from a previous portfolio peak to a later low point.

Example:

- Portfolio peak: USD 10,000
- Portfolio low: USD 7,500
- Drawdown: 25%

The portfolio must then gain more than 25% to return to its original value.

From USD 7,500, a gain of approximately 33.3% is required to return to USD 10,000.

## Why Recovery Requires a Larger Percentage

Loss and recovery percentages are not symmetrical.

| Loss | Gain Needed to Recover |
|---:|---:|
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 20% | 25.0% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 40% | 66.7% |
| 50% | 100.0% |

This is one reason risk control matters.

## Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is an investor’s willingness and emotional comfort with uncertain returns and temporary losses.

Questions include:

- Would I panic after a 10% decline?
- Could I tolerate a 20% decline?
- Would I sell after a 30% decline?
- Would a temporary loss affect essential household spending?
- Would I need the invested money during a market downturn?

## Risk Capacity

Risk capacity is different from risk tolerance.

Risk capacity is the financial ability to absorb investment losses without jeopardizing essential spending, near-term obligations, or major financial goals.

Risk capacity depends on financial circumstances, including:

- Age
- Income stability
- Pension income
- Emergency savings
- Debt
- Health expenses
- Dependents
- Investment horizon
- Need for regular withdrawals

A person may emotionally tolerate risk but lack the financial capacity to absorb losses.

## Time Horizon

The time horizon is how long money can remain invested before it is needed.

Shorter time horizons generally require greater attention to:

- Capital preservation
- Liquidity
- Volatility
- Interest-rate risk
- Sequence-of-returns risk

Money needed soon should not be treated the same as money intended for long-term growth.

## Sequence-of-Returns Risk

Sequence-of-returns risk is the danger of suffering large losses early while also withdrawing money.

This is particularly important for retirees.

Two portfolios can earn the same average return but produce very different outcomes if losses occur at different times.

Early losses combined with withdrawals can permanently reduce the portfolio's ability to recover.

## Emergency Reserve

Before investing, maintain an emergency reserve for:

- Housing
- Food
- Utilities
- Medical expenses
- Insurance
- Transportation
- Unexpected family needs

Emergency money should be accessible and should not depend on selling a volatile ETF during a market decline.

## Concentration Checklist

Before considering an ETF, review:

- Largest holding
- Top 10 holdings
- Sector percentages
- Country percentages
- Currency exposure
- Index methodology
- Market-cap weighting
- Overlap with existing investments
- Bond credit quality
- Bond duration
- Dependence on one economic theme

## Personal Risk Exercise

Answer these questions:

1. What is the largest temporary decline I believe I could tolerate?
2. What decline could I financially afford?
3. How many months of household expenses are held outside investments?
4. Will any invested money be needed within three years?
5. What percentage of household spending is in Colombian pesos?
6. What percentage of income is received in US dollars?
7. Would a falling US dollar create financial pressure?
8. Would a falling Colombian peso create financial pressure?
9. Do I need current income, growth, capital preservation, or a combination?
10. How would I respond emotionally to a 25% portfolio decline?

## Beginner Rules

1. Do not confuse many holdings with true diversification.
2. Check overlap before buying multiple ETFs.
3. Do not place emergency savings in volatile investments.
4. Do not invest money needed for near-term expenses.
5. Review sector, country, and currency concentration.
6. Understand that diversified equity ETFs can still experience major losses.
7. Evaluate both risk tolerance and risk capacity.
8. Consider withdrawal needs before selecting an allocation.
9. Avoid leveraged and inverse ETFs while learning.
10. Record the reasons for every investment decision.
