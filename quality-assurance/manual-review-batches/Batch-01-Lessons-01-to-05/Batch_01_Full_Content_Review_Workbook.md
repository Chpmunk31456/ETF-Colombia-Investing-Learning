# Lesson 1 — What Is an ETF?

## Learning Objective

Understand what an exchange-traded fund is, how it works, and how it differs from an individual stock and a traditional mutual fund.

## Definition

An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, is an investment fund that owns a collection of assets and trades on a stock exchange.

Depending on its objective, an ETF may hold:

- Stocks
- Government or corporate bonds
- Real-estate securities
- Commodities
- Cash-equivalent instruments
- A combination of asset classes

When an investor buys one ETF share, the investor receives economic exposure to the collection of investments held by that fund.

## Simple Example

A broad stock-market ETF might own shares in hundreds or thousands of companies.

Instead of selecting and buying each company separately, an investor can purchase one ETF that tracks the broader market.

## ETF Versus Individual Stock

### Individual stock

An individual stock represents ownership in one company.

Its performance depends heavily on that company’s:

- Revenue
- Profitability
- Management
- Debt
- Industry
- Competitive position
- Legal and regulatory risks

### ETF

An ETF usually spreads money across multiple holdings.

This can reduce company-specific risk, although it does not eliminate market risk.

## ETF Versus Mutual Fund

Both ETFs and mutual funds can pool investor money into diversified portfolios.

Important differences may include:

| Feature | ETF | Traditional Mutual Fund |
|---|---|---|
| Trading | During market hours | Usually priced once daily |
| Price | Changes throughout the day | Based on end-of-day net asset value |
| Minimum investment | Often one share or fractional share | May require a minimum deposit |
| Bid-ask spread | Usually applies | Usually does not apply directly |
| Expense ratio | Varies | Varies |
| Brokerage commission | May apply | May have purchase or redemption fees |

## Index ETF

An index ETF attempts to track a defined market index.

Examples of index categories include:

- Large US companies
- The total US stock market
- Global developed markets
- Emerging markets
- Government bonds
- Corporate bonds

An index ETF does not necessarily attempt to select the “best” investments. Its objective is generally to reproduce the performance of its selected index, before fees and tracking differences.

## Actively Managed ETF

An actively managed ETF uses investment managers to select holdings based on a strategy.

Active management may:

- Cost more
- Trade more frequently
- Perform differently from the broad market
- Outperform or underperform its benchmark

## Main Advantages

Potential advantages include:

- Diversification
- Transparent market pricing
- Relatively easy buying and selling
- Access to broad markets
- Often lower costs than some actively managed funds
- Ability to invest small amounts

## Main Risks

ETFs are not guaranteed investments.

Important risks include:

- Market losses
- Currency fluctuations
- Interest-rate risk
- Credit risk
- Concentration risk
- Liquidity risk
- Tracking error
- Tax consequences
- Brokerage and currency-conversion costs

## Important Terms

### Expense ratio

The annual percentage of fund assets used to pay operating expenses.

### Bid price

The highest current price a buyer is offering.

### Ask price

The lowest current price a seller is requesting.

### Bid-ask spread

The difference between the bid and ask prices.

### Net asset value

The estimated value of the fund’s underlying assets minus liabilities.

### Tracking error

The difference between the ETF’s performance and the performance of its intended benchmark.

### Assets under management

The total market value of assets managed by the fund.

## Beginner Rule

Do not select an ETF based only on:

- Recent performance
- Social-media popularity
- Dividend yield
- An AI recommendation
- A low share price
- A famous company name

The fund’s objective, holdings, costs, risks, legal structure, tax treatment, and currency exposure must be understood first.

# Lesson 2 — ETF Costs and Trading Mechanics

## Learning Objective

Understand the main direct and indirect costs of owning an ETF and how trading mechanics can affect the actual price paid.

## Why Costs Matter

Investment returns are uncertain, but costs are real.

Even a small annual fee can reduce long-term returns because the money paid in fees no longer compounds for the investor.

ETF costs are not limited to the expense ratio. A complete review should include:

- Expense ratio
- Brokerage commission
- Bid-ask spread
- Currency-conversion cost
- Custody or platform fees
- Tax costs
- Tracking difference
- Possible withdrawal or transfer fees

## Expense Ratio

The expense ratio is the annual percentage of fund assets used to pay the ETF's operating expenses.

Example:

If an ETF has an expense ratio of 0.20%, the annual fund cost is approximately:

- USD 2 for every USD 1,000 invested
- USD 20 for every USD 10,000 invested
- USD 200 for every USD 100,000 invested

The fee is normally deducted inside the fund. It is not usually charged as a separate invoice.

## Expense Ratio Is Not the Total Cost

Two ETFs can have the same expense ratio but different total investor costs.

Possible differences include:

- Wider or narrower bid-ask spreads
- Better or worse tracking
- Different brokerage commissions
- Different tax treatment
- Different currency-conversion fees
- Different liquidity
- Different withholding-tax exposure

## Bid and Ask Prices

### Bid

The bid is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.

### Ask

The ask is the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.

### Spread

The bid-ask spread is the difference between those two prices.

Example:

- Bid: USD 99.95
- Ask: USD 100.05
- Spread: USD 0.10

A narrower spread is generally better for the investor because less value is lost when entering or exiting a position.

## Market Orders

A market order instructs the broker to execute the trade at the best price currently available.

Advantages:

- Usually executes quickly
- Simple to use

Risks:

- Final execution price may differ from the displayed price
- Can be costly in thinly traded ETFs
- Can produce unfavorable results during volatile markets
- Can be risky near market opening or closing

## Limit Orders

A limit order sets the maximum purchase price or minimum sale price the investor will accept.

Example:

An investor may place a buy limit order at USD 100.00.

The order will execute only if shares are available at USD 100.00 or lower.

Advantages:

- More control over execution price
- Helps reduce unexpected price slippage

Risks:

- The order may not execute
- A partially filled order may occur
- The market can move away from the limit price

## Liquidity

ETF liquidity can be evaluated through:

- Average daily trading volume
- Bid-ask spread
- Assets under management
- Liquidity of the underlying securities
- Number and quality of market makers

High trading volume can be helpful, but the liquidity of the underlying assets also matters.

A bond ETF or international ETF may have lower visible trading volume while still having meaningful underlying liquidity.

## Tracking Difference

Tracking difference is the difference between the ETF's actual return and the return of its benchmark.

An ETF may trail its benchmark because of:

- Expense ratio
- Trading costs
- Taxes
- Sampling methods
- Cash holdings
- Rebalancing delays
- Securities-lending practices

A lower expense ratio does not always guarantee better benchmark tracking.

## Currency-Conversion Costs

An investor living in Colombia may earn or hold money in Colombian pesos but buy an ETF priced in US dollars.

Potential costs include:

- COP-to-USD conversion spread
- Bank transfer fees
- Brokerage conversion fees
- International wire fees
- Withdrawal conversion costs

These costs should be measured before investing.

## Platform and Custody Fees

A broker may charge:

- Monthly account fees
- Inactivity fees
- Custody fees
- Data fees
- Withdrawal fees
- Foreign-market access fees
- Transfer-out fees

A "commission-free" trade does not necessarily mean the account is free.

## Taxes and Withholding

Possible tax-related costs may include:

- Dividend withholding
- Capital-gains tax
- Colombian tax reporting
- US tax reporting
- Foreign tax-credit limitations
- Estate-tax considerations
- PFIC reporting for certain non-US pooled funds

Tax treatment can materially change the real return.

## Comparing Two ETFs

When comparing two ETFs, record:

| Category | ETF A | ETF B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Expense ratio |  |  |
| Bid-ask spread |  |  |
| Average daily volume |  |  |
| Assets under management |  |  |
| Tracking difference |  |  |
| Brokerage commission |  |  |
| Currency-conversion cost |  |  |
| Dividend withholding |  |  |
| Other platform fees |  |  |

## Beginner Trading Rules

1. Understand the ETF before placing an order.
2. Check the bid and ask prices.
3. Prefer limit orders when price control matters.
4. Avoid trading during unusually volatile conditions.
5. Avoid buying immediately at market open without understanding the risks.
6. Confirm the correct ticker and exchange.
7. Review currency-conversion costs.
8. Record the actual execution price.
9. Keep official confirmations and tax records.
10. Never assume "commission-free" means cost-free.

# Lesson 3 — Diversification, Risk, and Drawdowns

## Learning Objective

Understand what diversification can and cannot do, how ETF risk should be evaluated, and why temporary losses are a normal part of investing.

## What Diversification Means

Diversification means spreading investment exposure across multiple holdings, sectors, countries, issuers, or asset classes.

The purpose is to reduce the impact of one investment performing poorly.

Diversification does not guarantee a profit and does not prevent losses during a broad market decline.

## Diversification Within One ETF

An ETF may appear diversified because it owns many securities, but the number of holdings alone is not enough.

A fund with 500 holdings may still be concentrated if:

- Its largest companies dominate the portfolio
- Most holdings are in one country
- One sector represents a large percentage
- Holdings respond similarly to the same economic risks
- The index uses market-cap weighting
- Several companies are controlled by the same industry trends

## Diversification Across ETFs

Owning several ETFs does not automatically create more diversification.

Two ETFs may hold many of the same companies.

This is called portfolio overlap.

Example:

- ETF A tracks large US companies
- ETF B tracks a technology-heavy index
- ETF C tracks US growth companies

All three may have large positions in the same technology companies.

The investor may believe the portfolio is diversified while actually increasing concentration.

## Types of Diversification

### Company diversification

Exposure is spread among different companies.

### Sector diversification

Exposure is spread among industries such as:

- Technology
- Healthcare
- Financial services
- Consumer goods
- Energy
- Industrials
- Utilities

### Geographic diversification

Exposure is spread among:

- United States
- Developed international markets
- Emerging markets
- Colombia
- Other regions

### Asset-class diversification

Exposure is spread among:

- Stocks
- Bonds
- Cash
- Real estate
- Other assets

### Currency diversification

Exposure is spread among currencies such as:

- Colombian peso
- US dollar
- Euro
- Other currencies

Currency diversification can reduce dependence on one currency, but it also introduces exchange-rate risk.

## Market Risk

Market risk is the possibility that an entire market declines.

A diversified stock ETF can still lose substantial value during:

- Recessions
- Financial crises
- Wars
- Pandemics
- Interest-rate shocks
- Political instability
- Market bubbles
- Sudden changes in investor confidence

Diversification reduces some risks but does not eliminate market risk.

## Company-Specific Risk

Company-specific risk is the possibility that one business performs poorly because of:

- Management failure
- Fraud
- Product failure
- Debt problems
- Lawsuits
- Regulation
- Competition
- Technological disruption

A broad ETF usually reduces this risk because no single company represents the entire investment.

## Sector Risk

Sector risk occurs when a particular industry performs poorly.

Examples include:

- Technology valuations falling
- Energy prices collapsing
- Banks suffering credit losses
- Healthcare firms facing regulation
- Real-estate securities declining when rates rise

A sector ETF may be diversified among companies but still concentrated in one economic risk.

## Country Risk

Country risk includes:

- Political instability
- Currency controls
- Tax changes
- Regulatory changes
- Economic recession
- Sovereign debt problems
- Restrictions on capital movement

A Colombia-focused investment may be exposed to Colombian economic and currency risks.

A foreign ETF may reduce country concentration but introduce other legal, tax, and currency risks.

## Currency Risk

Currency risk occurs when an investment and the investor's spending needs use different currencies.

Example:

- The ETF is priced in US dollars
- Household expenses are paid in Colombian pesos

The investment return measured in pesos depends on:

1. The ETF's market return
2. The change in the USD/COP exchange rate
3. Currency-conversion costs

A rising dollar can increase the peso value of a US investment.

A falling dollar can reduce the peso value, even when the ETF itself gains in dollars.

## Interest-Rate Risk

Interest-rate risk is especially important for bond ETFs.

When market interest rates rise, existing bond prices often fall.

Long-duration bond ETFs are generally more sensitive to interest-rate changes than short-duration bond ETFs.

## Credit Risk

Credit risk is the possibility that a bond issuer cannot make scheduled interest or principal payments.

Government bond ETFs and corporate bond ETFs can have different levels of credit risk.

Higher yield usually comes with higher risk.

## Volatility

Volatility measures how much an investment's price moves over time.

High volatility means prices may rise or fall sharply.

Low volatility does not mean no risk.

An investment may appear stable for years and still suffer a large decline under unusual conditions.

## Drawdown

A drawdown is the decline from a previous portfolio peak to a later low point.

Example:

- Portfolio peak: USD 10,000
- Portfolio low: USD 7,500
- Drawdown: 25%

The portfolio must then gain more than 25% to return to its original value.

From USD 7,500, a gain of approximately 33.3% is required to return to USD 10,000.

## Why Recovery Requires a Larger Percentage

Loss and recovery percentages are not symmetrical.

| Loss | Gain Needed to Recover |
|---:|---:|
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 20% | 25.0% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 40% | 66.7% |
| 50% | 100.0% |

This is one reason risk control matters.

## Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is the emotional and financial ability to accept investment losses.

Questions include:

- Would I panic after a 10% decline?
- Could I tolerate a 20% decline?
- Would I sell after a 30% decline?
- Would a temporary loss affect essential household spending?
- Would I need the invested money during a market downturn?

## Risk Capacity

Risk capacity is different from risk tolerance.

Risk capacity depends on financial circumstances, including:

- Age
- Income stability
- Pension income
- Emergency savings
- Debt
- Health expenses
- Dependents
- Investment horizon
- Need for regular withdrawals

A person may emotionally tolerate risk but lack the financial capacity to absorb losses.

## Time Horizon

The time horizon is how long money can remain invested before it is needed.

Shorter time horizons generally require greater attention to:

- Capital preservation
- Liquidity
- Volatility
- Interest-rate risk
- Sequence-of-returns risk

Money needed soon should not be treated the same as money intended for long-term growth.

## Sequence-of-Returns Risk

Sequence-of-returns risk is the danger of suffering large losses early while also withdrawing money.

This is particularly important for retirees.

Two portfolios can earn the same average return but produce very different outcomes if losses occur at different times.

Early losses combined with withdrawals can permanently reduce the portfolio's ability to recover.

## Emergency Reserve

Before investing, maintain an emergency reserve for:

- Housing
- Food
- Utilities
- Medical expenses
- Insurance
- Transportation
- Unexpected family needs

Emergency money should be accessible and should not depend on selling a volatile ETF during a market decline.

## Concentration Checklist

Before considering an ETF, review:

- Largest holding
- Top 10 holdings
- Sector percentages
- Country percentages
- Currency exposure
- Index methodology
- Market-cap weighting
- Overlap with existing investments
- Bond credit quality
- Bond duration
- Dependence on one economic theme

## Personal Risk Exercise

Answer these questions:

1. What is the largest temporary decline I believe I could tolerate?
2. What decline could I financially afford?
3. How many months of household expenses are held outside investments?
4. Will any invested money be needed within three years?
5. What percentage of household spending is in Colombian pesos?
6. What percentage of income is received in US dollars?
7. Would a falling US dollar create financial pressure?
8. Would a falling Colombian peso create financial pressure?
9. Do I need current income, growth, capital preservation, or a combination?
10. How would I respond emotionally to a 25% portfolio decline?

## Beginner Rules

1. Do not confuse many holdings with true diversification.
2. Check overlap before buying multiple ETFs.
3. Do not place emergency savings in volatile investments.
4. Do not invest money needed for near-term expenses.
5. Review sector, country, and currency concentration.
6. Understand that diversified equity ETFs can still experience major losses.
7. Evaluate both risk tolerance and risk capacity.
8. Consider withdrawal needs before selecting an allocation.
9. Avoid leveraged and inverse ETFs while learning.
10. Record the reasons for every investment decision.

7. How can currency movements affect a Colombia-based investor?
8. Why should emergency savings remain outside volatile investments?

# Lesson 4 — ETF Types and Index Construction

## Learning Objective

Understand the major ETF categories, how indexes are constructed, and why two ETFs that sound similar may behave very differently.

## Why ETF Classification Matters

The word "ETF" describes the legal and trading structure of a fund. It does not describe the fund's risk.

Two ETFs can both trade on an exchange while having completely different:

- Holdings
- Volatility
- Income
- Currency exposure
- Tax treatment
- Liquidity
- Expected return
- Downside risk

The investment strategy must be understood before purchasing the fund.

## Broad-Market Equity ETFs

Broad-market equity ETFs invest across a large segment of the stock market.

Examples of broad categories include:

- Large US companies
- Total US stock market
- Developed international markets
- Emerging markets
- Global stocks
- Colombian equities

Potential advantages:

- Broad diversification
- Simple portfolio construction
- Lower company-specific risk
- Often relatively low fees

Important risks:

- Broad market declines
- Country concentration
- Sector concentration
- Currency risk
- Market-cap concentration

## Bond ETFs

Bond ETFs hold portfolios of debt securities.

Possible categories include:

- Government bonds
- Investment-grade corporate bonds
- High-yield corporate bonds
- Municipal bonds
- Inflation-linked bonds
- Short-term bonds
- Long-term bonds
- Emerging-market bonds

Important variables include:

- Duration
- Maturity
- Credit quality
- Yield
- Currency
- Issuer concentration
- Interest-rate sensitivity

Bond ETFs can lose value. They are not equivalent to insured bank deposits or a CDT held to maturity.

## Dividend ETFs

Dividend ETFs select companies based on dividend-related rules.

Different strategies may emphasize:

- High current yield
- Dividend growth
- Dividend consistency
- Financial strength
- Low volatility

Potential risks:

- Concentration in mature sectors
- Dividend reductions
- Underperformance during growth-led markets
- High yield caused by falling share prices
- Tax withholding on distributions

A high dividend yield is not automatically a sign of quality.

## Sector ETFs

Sector ETFs focus on one industry or economic segment.

Examples include:

- Technology
- Healthcare
- Financial services
- Energy
- Utilities
- Consumer staples
- Industrials
- Real estate

Potential advantage:

- Targeted exposure to a specific investment view

Main risk:

- Heavy concentration in one industry

Sector ETFs should not be treated as broadly diversified core holdings without careful analysis.

## Thematic ETFs

Thematic ETFs invest around a narrative or long-term trend.

Examples may include:

- Artificial intelligence
- Cybersecurity
- Robotics
- Clean energy
- Electric vehicles
- Space technology
- Biotechnology
- Blockchain

Potential risks:

- High fees
- Marketing-driven demand
- Overlapping holdings
- Unprofitable companies
- High valuations
- Short operating history
- Theme definitions that vary by provider

A compelling story does not guarantee attractive investment returns.

## Commodity ETFs

Commodity ETFs may provide exposure to:

- Gold
- Silver
- Oil
- Natural gas
- Agricultural commodities
- Broad commodity indexes

They may hold:

- Physical commodities
- Futures contracts
- Commodity-producing companies
- Derivative instruments

Important risks include:

- Futures roll costs
- Commodity-price volatility
- Storage and insurance costs
- Tracking differences
- Complex tax treatment

## Real-Estate ETFs

Real-estate ETFs often invest in listed real-estate investment trusts or property companies.

Potential benefits:

- Access to income-producing real estate
- Diversification across properties
- Easier liquidity than direct property ownership

Important risks:

- Interest-rate sensitivity
- Property-market downturns
- Tenant and occupancy risk
- Sector concentration
- Dividend taxation

## Currency ETFs

Currency ETFs provide exposure to foreign currencies or currency indexes.

They may use:

- Bank deposits
- Futures
- Forward contracts
- Other derivatives

These products can be complex and are generally not necessary for a beginner portfolio.

## Leveraged ETFs

Leveraged ETFs attempt to deliver a multiple of the daily return of an index.

Examples may target:

- Two times the daily return
- Three times the daily return

They typically reset daily.

Because of compounding effects, the return over longer periods can differ substantially from the stated daily multiple.

Main risks:

- Rapid losses
- Volatility decay
- Path dependency
- Derivative exposure
- Unsuitability for long-term holding

## Inverse ETFs

Inverse ETFs attempt to rise when an index falls, usually on a daily basis.

They may be used by traders for short-term hedging or speculation.

Main risks:

- Daily reset
- Compounding effects
- Rapid losses when markets rise
- High fees
- Poor long-term tracking

## Covered-Call ETFs

Covered-call ETFs hold securities and sell call options against them.

Potential advantages:

- Higher current distributions
- Some income during sideways markets

Important disadvantages:

- Limited upside participation
- Continued downside exposure
- Option-premium distributions may be misunderstood
- Complex tax treatment
- High headline yield may not equal strong total return

## Equal-Weight ETFs

Equal-weight ETFs assign similar weight to each holding.

Potential effects:

- Less dominance by the largest companies
- Greater exposure to smaller constituents
- More frequent rebalancing
- Higher turnover
- Different sector exposure

Equal weighting is not automatically safer than market-cap weighting.

## Market-Capitalization Weighting

Market-cap-weighted indexes assign larger weights to companies with greater market value.

Potential advantages:

- Low turnover
- Reflects the market's current valuation
- Scales naturally as companies grow or shrink

Potential disadvantages:

- Concentration in the largest companies
- Greater exposure to highly valued companies
- Sector concentration during market cycles

## Price Weighting

A price-weighted index gives more influence to stocks with higher share prices.

The share price itself, rather than the company's total market value, determines the weight.

This methodology can produce unintuitive results.

## Fundamental Weighting

Fundamentally weighted indexes use financial measures such as:

- Revenue
- Earnings
- Book value
- Dividends
- Cash flow

These strategies may differ materially from broad market-cap indexes.

## Factor ETFs

Factor ETFs select securities based on measurable characteristics.

Common factors include:

- Value
- Growth
- Quality
- Momentum
- Size
- Low volatility
- Dividend yield

Factor strategies can underperform for long periods.

An investor should understand:

- Factor definition
- Selection rules
- Rebalancing frequency
- Turnover
- Fees
- Historical periods of underperformance

## Sampling Versus Full Replication

### Full replication

The ETF holds all or nearly all securities in the index.

### Sampling

The ETF holds a representative subset of securities.

Sampling may be used when:

- The index contains thousands of securities
- Some securities are difficult to trade
- Transaction costs would be excessive

Sampling can increase tracking difference.

## Index Rebalancing

Indexes periodically update their holdings and weights.

Rebalancing may occur:

- Monthly
- Quarterly
- Semiannually
- Annually
- When special events occur

Frequent rebalancing can increase:

- Turnover
- Trading costs
- Tax effects
- Tracking differences

## Index Reconstitution

Reconstitution is the process of adding or removing securities based on the index rules.

A fund may be forced to buy securities after they rise or sell securities after they fall, depending on the methodology.

## Questions to Ask About an Index

Before evaluating an ETF, determine:

1. What market does the index represent?
2. How are securities selected?
3. How are holdings weighted?
4. How often is the index rebalanced?
5. How often is it reconstituted?
6. Are there concentration limits?
7. Are profitability or liquidity screens used?
8. Are foreign securities included?
9. How are dividends treated?
10. Does the ETF fully replicate or sample the index?

## Core Versus Satellite Approach

A common educational framework is:

### Core

Broad, diversified, lower-cost holdings intended to represent most of the portfolio.

### Satellite

Smaller, more specialized holdings used for:

- A sector view
- A factor strategy
- Income
- A specific investment theme

This framework does not guarantee success, but it can help prevent specialized ETFs from dominating the portfolio.

## Beginner Rules

1. Understand the ETF category before reviewing performance.
2. Read the index methodology.
3. Do not assume two broad-market ETFs are identical.
4. Avoid leveraged and inverse ETFs while learning.
5. Treat sector and thematic ETFs as concentrated products.
6. Evaluate total return, not dividend yield alone.
7. Check whether the ETF uses physical holdings, sampling, futures, or derivatives.
8. Review index weighting and concentration.
9. Compare overlapping holdings before combining ETFs.
10. Prefer understandable products over complex strategies.

# Lesson 5 — Colombia Investment Landscape

## Learning Objective

Understand the main investment products available to a person living in Colombia and how they differ in risk, liquidity, currency exposure, regulation, and complexity.

## Important Principle

The name of an investment does not determine whether it is suitable.

Before investing, evaluate:

- Purpose
- Expected holding period
- Liquidity
- Credit risk
- Market risk
- Currency exposure
- Fees
- Tax treatment
- Regulatory status
- Protection of client assets
- Consequences of early withdrawal

## Main Investment Categories in Colombia

Common categories include:

- Savings accounts
- High-yield savings products
- CDTs
- Colombian government bonds
- Corporate bonds
- Collective investment funds
- Voluntary pension funds
- Colombian stocks
- International stocks and ETFs
- Mercado Global Colombiano securities
- Real-estate investment vehicles
- Direct real estate
- Foreign brokerage accounts

Each category serves a different purpose.

## Savings Accounts

Savings accounts are primarily cash-management tools.

Potential advantages:

- High liquidity
- Easy access
- Familiar banking structure
- Suitable for short-term expenses and emergency reserves

Potential disadvantages:

- Returns may not keep pace with inflation
- Promotional rates may change
- Account fees may reduce returns
- Interest is generally taxable

Questions to ask:

1. Is the institution supervised?
2. Is deposit insurance applicable?
3. What fees apply?
4. Is the advertised rate temporary?
5. Is there a minimum balance?
6. How quickly can funds be withdrawn?

## CDTs

A CDT is a fixed-term deposit issued by a financial institution.

The investor agrees to leave money deposited for a specified period in exchange for interest.

Common terms may include:

- 30 days
- 90 days
- 180 days
- 360 days
- Longer periods

Potential advantages:

- Predictable contractual rate
- Simpler than market-traded securities
- Useful for known future expenses
- May offer higher rates than ordinary savings accounts

Potential disadvantages:

- Limited access before maturity
- Reinvestment risk
- Inflation risk
- Institution-specific credit exposure
- Early redemption may be difficult or unavailable
- Returns may be less attractive after taxes and inflation

## CDT Ladder

A CDT ladder divides money among several maturity dates.

Example:

- Portion 1: 90-day CDT
- Portion 2: 180-day CDT
- Portion 3: 270-day CDT
- Portion 4: 360-day CDT

As each deposit matures, the investor can:

- Use the money
- Reinvest it
- Adjust to new interest rates
- Preserve periodic liquidity

A ladder does not eliminate inflation, credit, or reinvestment risk.

## Colombian Government Bonds

Government bonds represent debt issued by the Colombian government.

They may include fixed-rate, inflation-linked, or other structures.

Potential advantages:

- Access to sovereign fixed income
- Potential income
- Different maturity options
- Possible diversification from equities

Important risks:

- Interest-rate risk
- Inflation risk
- Market-price volatility
- Sovereign risk
- Liquidity risk
- Currency risk for investors measuring wealth in US dollars

A government bond can lose market value before maturity.

## Corporate Bonds

Corporate bonds represent loans to companies.

Potential advantages:

- Income
- Higher yields than some government debt
- Diversification among issuers and sectors

Important risks:

- Default risk
- Credit-rating changes
- Interest-rate risk
- Liquidity risk
- Issuer concentration
- Complex covenants

Higher yield usually reflects higher risk.

## Collective Investment Funds

Collective investment funds pool money from multiple investors.

They may invest in:

- Cash instruments
- Fixed income
- Equities
- Real estate
- International securities
- Mixed portfolios

Potential advantages:

- Professional management
- Diversification
- Access to markets that may be difficult to enter individually
- Different risk profiles

Potential disadvantages:

- Management fees
- Entry or withdrawal restrictions
- Performance fees
- Market risk
- Possible complexity
- Tax consequences
- Limited transparency in some products

## Questions for Any Collective Fund

Ask:

1. What assets does it hold?
2. Who manages the fund?
3. Who acts as custodian?
4. What is the risk classification?
5. What fees are charged?
6. How often can money be withdrawn?
7. Is there a minimum holding period?
8. Is there a withdrawal penalty?
9. How has the fund behaved during market stress?
10. Are returns reported before or after fees?

## Voluntary Pension Funds

Voluntary pension products may provide long-term investment and tax-planning features.

Potential advantages:

- Long-term saving discipline
- Professional portfolio management
- Possible tax benefits under qualifying conditions
- Beneficiary planning

Potential disadvantages:

- Withdrawal restrictions
- Conditions attached to tax benefits
- Management fees
- Investment risk
- Complex cross-border tax treatment
- Possible US reporting complications for US citizens

A US citizen should obtain cross-border tax guidance before using a non-US pooled investment or pension vehicle.

## Colombian Stocks

A stock represents ownership in a publicly traded company.

Potential advantages:

- Growth potential
- Dividend income
- Direct participation in Colombian businesses
- Exposure to the Colombian economy

Important risks:

- Company-specific risk
- Sector concentration
- Political and regulatory risk
- Currency risk
- Lower liquidity in some securities
- Dividend variability
- Corporate-governance risk

The Colombian equity market may be more concentrated than larger international markets.

## Mercado Global Colombiano

The Mercado Global Colombiano provides access through Colombian market infrastructure to certain foreign securities.

Potential advantages:

- Local-market access
- Potentially simpler funding in Colombia
- Access to selected international companies or funds
- Local brokerage relationship

Potential disadvantages:

- Limited security selection
- Lower liquidity than the original foreign exchange
- Bid-ask spreads
- Currency exposure
- Foreign-asset reporting questions
- Tax treatment that may differ from investor assumptions

Buying a foreign security through Colombian infrastructure does not necessarily make it a Colombian asset for tax purposes.

## International ETFs

International ETFs may provide exposure to:

- US stocks
- Global stocks
- Bonds
- Real estate
- Commodities
- Specific sectors

A Colombia-based investor must consider:

- Fund domicile
- Trading currency
- Underlying currency exposure
- Brokerage costs
- Dividend withholding
- Colombian reporting
- US reporting
- Estate-tax exposure
- PFIC risk for non-US funds

## Foreign Brokerage Accounts

A foreign brokerage may offer broader access to international stocks and ETFs.

Potential advantages:

- Larger product selection
- Potentially lower trading costs
- Access to highly liquid markets
- Fractional shares at some brokers

Potential disadvantages:

- International transfer costs
- Currency conversion
- Tax reporting
- Estate issues
- Foreign-account reporting
- Customer-support challenges
- Jurisdiction and investor-protection differences
- Difficulty transferring money back to Colombia

## Real-Estate Investment Vehicles

Real-estate investment funds or listed property vehicles may provide exposure to:

- Offices
- Warehouses
- Retail properties
- Housing
- Hotels
- Infrastructure-related property

Potential advantages:

- Income potential
- Diversification
- Professional management
- Easier access than buying an entire property

Important risks:

- Property-market cycles
- Interest rates
- Tenant defaults
- Vacancy
- Valuation uncertainty
- Management fees
- Limited liquidity

## Direct Real Estate

Direct property ownership differs significantly from owning a financial security.

Potential advantages:

- Tangible asset
- Rental income
- Possible inflation protection
- Personal use

Potential disadvantages:

- Large initial capital requirement
- Low liquidity
- Maintenance
- Taxes
- HOA or administration fees
- Vacancy
- Tenant risk
- Legal expenses
- Concentration in one property and location

## COP Versus USD Exposure

A Colombia-based investor may receive income in US dollars while spending in Colombian pesos.

This creates important planning questions:

- How much should remain in COP for near-term expenses?
- How much USD exposure is appropriate?
- What happens if the peso strengthens?
- What happens if the peso weakens?
- What are the conversion costs?
- Which currency matches future liabilities?

Currency exposure should be intentional, not accidental.

## Liquidity Categories

### Immediate liquidity

Examples:

- Savings accounts
- Money-market-like products

### Short-term liquidity

Examples:

- Short CDTs
- Short-duration fixed-income funds

### Market liquidity

Examples:

- Public stocks
- ETFs
- Listed bonds

These can usually be sold, but the price may be unfavorable.

### Limited liquidity

Examples:

- Long-term funds
- Private investments
- Direct real estate
- Certain pension products

## Regulatory Verification

Before sending money:

1. Identify the exact legal entity.
2. Verify the institution through the relevant regulator.
3. Confirm that the website and contact details are official.
4. Understand where client assets are held.
5. Review withdrawal procedures.
6. Confirm investor-protection arrangements.
7. Avoid transferring money to an individual.
8. Avoid guaranteed-return claims.
9. Never share brokerage passwords.
10. Enable multi-factor authentication.

## Preliminary Suitability Framework

### Emergency reserve

Priority:

- Capital preservation
- Immediate access
- Low complexity

### Money needed within three years

Priority:

- Liquidity
- Low volatility
- Known maturity dates
- Limited market exposure

### Long-term growth capital

Possible considerations:

- Diversified equity exposure
- Bond exposure
- Currency diversification
- Controlled volatility

### Income-oriented capital

Possible considerations:

- Fixed income
- CDTs
- Dividend-paying investments
- Distribution reliability
- Tax effects

Income should be evaluated using total return and risk, not yield alone.
